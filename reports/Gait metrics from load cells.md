# Trust the load, caveat the gait

Four vertical load cells at 940 Hz under a single deck can produce far more than most engineers expect — every temporal gait event, the full centre-of-pressure trajectory, per-limb vertical force via published decomposition, impulse, cumulative load and every symmetry index built on those — but almost none of it is clinically validated, and the one thing this hardware does best is the one thing the literature says clinicians desperately need. **Unaided partial weight-bearing compliance after total knee arthroplasty is 0% on day one and 2% at discharge, with 84–92% of patients overloading the limb on every single step** ([PMC10439061](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10439061/)), and real-time load biofeedback cuts overload steps to **≤8.4%** in randomised trials ([Sensors 24(19):6443](https://mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/19/6443/htm)). Against that, the APTA's total knee arthroplasty guideline recommends **no gait measure at all** ([APTA TKA CPG](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7462050)), there is **no accepted standard formula for gait symmetry and no validated symmetry cut-off** ([Patterson et al.](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1644239)), and **no published MDC or MCID exists for cadence, step length, symmetry, vertical impulse or loading rate** in any of this device's target populations. Compounding that, body-weight support flattens the vertical force waveform until the two peaks and the mid-stance trough become indistinguishable at 30% offload ([Barela et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/)), so the metrics that depend on waveform shape break in exactly the operating mode this machine exists for. The decision this report lands on is a short screen: measured weight-bearing load, delivered body-weight support, gait speed, vertical impulse with stance time, and per-limb load trends — with roughly a dozen plausible-looking metrics deliberately left off.

## Four vertical cells at 940 Hz are not the binding constraint

The vertical-only architecture costs less than it appears to. Centre of pressure is the point of application of the *vertical* force, so a four-corner deck recovers it exactly — `X_cop = ((F1+F2) − (F3+F4)) · (l/2) / ΣF` and the mediolateral equivalent, or the algebraically identical weighted-centroid form `X_cg = Σ(W_i·X_i)/ΣW_i`, which generalises to non-rectangular layouts ([PMC5375905](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5375905/)). Four cells recover three independent quantities (Fz, Mx, My) and therefore a 2D CoP, with one redundant reading available for least-squares calibration. What the deck forfeits is *horizontal force*, and with it braking and propulsive peaks and impulses, the anteroposterior weight-acceptance and propulsion variables, mediolateral force, the resultant GRF vector, the `F_X·dz` CoP correction, and any inverse-dynamics joint moment. Any claim about "push-off versus braking function" or about patellofemoral force, knee adduction moment or Achilles tendon load is structurally out of reach ([kinetic notes synthesis, GaitRec](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217853/)). That is a favourable trade: the accuracy hierarchy in the decomposition literature runs **vertical ≪ anteroposterior < mediolateral**, so a vertical-only deck operates in the best part of the error landscape and simply cannot attempt the components the algorithms handle badly anyway.

Limb attribution is the real architectural problem, and it is solved well enough. A single undivided deck gives two equations — vertical equilibrium and moment equilibrium about an AP axis — against four unknowns ([US9579044B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9579044B2/en)), so the per-limb split during double support is mathematically indeterminate without assumptions. The directly applicable solution is **Meurisse et al. 2016**: detect front-foot contact and back-foot off from the distance covered by the CoP, then reconstruct each limb's vertical force with a revised Davis–Cavanagh approach. Validated over **811 double-contact force measurements from 27 healthy subjects and 88 patients against individual force plates, it achieved median relative error of 1.8% (healthy) and 2.5% (clinical), with R² = 0.998 and 0.991** ([Meurisse 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966636215009133)), and a companion analysis reported mean absolute difference of 1.76% ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/264388878_Determination_of_vertical_ground_reaction_forces_under_each_foot_during_walking)). Raison's ΔCoP² thresholding — the squared Euclidean norm of inter-sample CoP displacement, thresholded at the CoP white-noise magnitude (~0.01 m) times a safety factor of 3 — replaces the original method's manual visual inspection of the CoP plot, and reported 5.0% mean relative error across 36 children's double stances ([US9579044B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9579044B2/en)). The methods that go further are all forfeited: Bastien's fore-aft stage takes total Fy as a model *predictor* and its lateral stage closes on a transverse-moment balance ([Bastien 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966636218319751)), Villeger's moment decomposition needs Mx/My/Mz, and Yu's physics-informed network consumes all five channels ([PubMed 40618708](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40618708/)). Build per-limb *vertical* force and per-limb CoP; do not claim per-limb 3D forces.

Sampling rate is a non-issue at 940 Hz. One sample is 1.06 ms, against a best-case threshold-detection error of roughly **9–10 ms median** with a 20 N threshold on clean per-limb force ([Rouhani et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25800003/); [Tirosh & Sparrow](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jab/19/2/article-p178.xml)), and against reference-grade practice of 1000 Hz with a 15 N threshold ([bioRxiv 2025](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.03.09.642234v1.full)). 940 Hz sits inside the reference band and leaves headroom for instantaneous loading rate, which is the only metric that genuinely needs ≥1000 Hz because it estimates a derivative from the single steepest inter-sample interval. Four engineering constraints matter more than the rate. First, **inter-channel skew appears directly as CoP noise**, because CoP is a ratio of simultaneously-sampled channels — simultaneous rather than multiplexed ADC sampling, with low clock jitter, is the design requirement, and jitter was flagged as a distinct validity threat independent of nominal rate ([PMC4239899](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4239899/)). Second, **real-time filter delay is unavoidable and must be budgeted**: a causal Butterworth delays different frequencies by different amounts, with a closed-form solution available for the delay ([J Biomech](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021929006000480)), and the 2024 real-time CoP algorithm attributed part of its error to exactly this. Third, **CoP is numerically explosive as ΣF → 0** and must be gated on a vertical-force threshold. Fourth, **the textbook 20 N threshold cannot be applied to the summed trace at all**, because total vGRF never returns to zero during walking double support — thresholding is only valid per-limb after decomposition, which makes CoP-transition logic the primary event detector, with the known belt speed as a strong prior since single-stance plate-frame CoP regresses at approximately belt speed ([treadmill CoP literature](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7722202_Determining_the_centre_of_pressure_during_walking_and_running_using_an_instrumented_treadmill)).

Three error budgets should be written into the console's own uncertainty figures. CoP-based foot-off is systematically **late** — 31 ms in Roerdink's 12-subject single-platform study against kinematics, and 111–126 ms in a real-time 200 Hz implementation ([Roerdink et al. 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021929008003199); [PMC11403786](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11403786/)) — so foot contact is the trustworthy event and foot-off wants a fixed offset correction. Step and stride length are not measured but reconstructed as `time × belt speed`, valid only if the patient does not drift fore-aft ([arXiv:1710.09030](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.09030)); the matched-speed head-to-head against a walkway found **temporal 95% limits of agreement typically under ±2% of gait cycle but spatial limits as wide as ±5 cm**, with every parameter significantly different (n = 39) ([Wearing, Reed & Urry](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966636212004729)). And the **mediolateral axis is the weak one** across three independent datasets: insole CoP path width agreed with force plates at only r = 0.11 ([DeBerardinis et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7338728)), load-cell ML CoP at r = 0.22 uphill ([Kim et al. 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12157732/)), and Villeger's lateral force RMSE was ~18%. Since CoP error scales as (l/2)·σ_F/ΣF, a long deck amplifies cell noise into AP CoP error while the short ML axis carries the smallest signal. Treat lateral symmetry and ML excursion as the least trustworthy outputs on this hardware.

The one failure mode with no literature at all is the one this device creates. Every decomposition family calibrates on a true single-support interval, and **CoP noise scales as 1/ΣF while the CoP event signal scales with inter-foot separation times the incoming foot's load fraction**. Very slow walking, shuffling gait, and body-weight support all attack the same quantity from different directions — and this machine is built to deliver slow, offloaded walking to guarded post-surgical patients, stacking all three. A patient who never fully unloads one limb breaks the method structurally rather than statistically: there is no interval in which the force is attributable to one limb, so the system never becomes determinate. Handrail use breaks it a second way, by injecting an unmeasured vertical force so that ΣFz no longer closes on body load and the "remainder assigned to the landing foot" step absorbs the handrail load as a one-limb force error. **No published study quantifies decomposition error below 0.4 m/s, under body-weight support, or with handrail use** — these are first-principles consequences, and they are the device's primary validation obligation.

## Unaided weight-bearing compliance is 0–2%, and that is the product

The single strongest evidence base touching this hardware is not gait analysis at all. In a prospective study of **50 TKA patients with instrumented insoles calibrated to each patient's body weight and a prescribed 200 N (~20 kg) limit, compliance — defined as overloading in under 10% of steps — was 0% on post-operative day one and 2% the day before discharge**; 84% overloaded on every step on day one, rising to 90% in the hallway and 92% on stairs at discharge. Mean load on the operated limb was **50 ± 14% of body weight on day one and 57 ± 17% at discharge**, with mean maximum load per step of 401 ± 140 N against 765 ± 174 N contralateral. Patients over 65 loaded the limb **17% more on day one and 34% more at discharge**. The authors call the 200 N protocol "senseless" and not feasible for most TKA patients, noting that crutches floor the load at roughly half body weight in younger patients ([PMC10439061](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10439061/)). Elderly proximal femoral fracture patients exceeded target in ≥72% of cases with **mean peak loading at 285% of target** ([Bradford Scholars](https://bradscholars.brad.ac.uk/entities/publication/3883d0e1-40e3-4eb2-9632-a2df907eea1e)), and healthy volunteers taught on bathroom scales produced **peak error at a 20% BW target double the intended load**, with error shrinking to non-significant at 80% ([Adelaide repository](https://web4.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/93259?mode=full)).

The prescription itself is terminologically unstable, which dictates the display format. A survey of **707 UK trauma and orthopaedic professionals found 89% interpret toe-touch weight bearing as 10% or 20% BW and 80% interpret partial weight bearing as 50%**, with doctors giving systematically higher values than therapists ([PubMed 29629184](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29629184/)); the published convention is TTWB ≈ 20% TBW or 10–15 kg and PWB 30–50% TBW, and the authors explicitly recommend never using the term without a numeric descriptor such as "PWB at 40%" ([BMJ Open SEM 4(1):e000326](https://bmjopensem.bmj.com/content/4/1/e000326.abstract)). Crucially, **percentage-based orders are followed more accurately than kilogram-based ones: 58% versus 40% success for TTWB and 50% versus 22% for PWB** in surgeon-simulated protocols ([Orthobullets evidence record](https://orthobullets.com/evidence/20954673)). Display %BW and N/kg simultaneously, never a bare category label.

Biofeedback works on this specific problem, with the strongest effect sizes in these notes. A randomised trial in healthy adults with a 15–30 kg target found that without feedback only one in two steps fell in the target zone and overloading occurred on at least one step in three, while **the biofeedback group reduced overload steps to ≤8.4% and the control group showed no significant change** ([Sensors 24(19):6443](https://mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/19/6443/htm)). A 60-participant trial with a 20 kg target found **mean peak force of 330 N with biofeedback versus 600 N in standard care, p ≤ 0.001** ([IJSPT](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/129259-the-impact-of-real-time-biofeedback-on-partial-weightbearing-training-a-comparative-study)). Three caveats belong on the record. The clean randomised trials are in **healthy volunteers**, not patients; the TKA patient studies are small and non-randomised, with one concluding only that correct partial weight bearing "is not obtainable without a measuring system" (n = 30, [Applied Sciences 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/5/2456)). Pfeufer's systematic review — **11 studies, 416 unilateral TKA patients, 9 of 11 reporting significant improvement in activity scores or pain** ([LMU record](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81236/)) — pools heterogeneous biofeedback modalities and is not evidence that *force* feedback improves TKA outcomes. And within-session symmetry gains from concurrent force feedback during leg press and squat **did not retain one week later** ([IJSPT](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/84312-concurrent-force-feedback-on-load-symmetry-in-total-knee-arthroplasty-patients)). The honest value proposition is in-session monitoring and guidance, not durable motor relearning — and no retrieved study establishes that exceeding a prescribed limit causes worse clinical outcomes, so the console informs a prescription whose own justification is contested.

Display design follows directly: **peak load per step and the proportion of steps in overload**, not a session mean, because "% of steps overloaded" was the clinically meaningful outcome and a mean masks per-step violations. Very low prescriptions should be surfaced as likely unachievable rather than rendered as a permanently failing target. And the console should state its own agreement with a force-plate reference, particularly above 50% BW, where validity testing found **no existing insole device achieved acceptable agreement in the >50–75% range** ([UMC Utrecht](https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/biofeedback-in-partial-weight-bearing-validity-of-3-different-dev/)).

## Gait speed is the only spatiotemporal metric with a defensible change threshold

The guideline landscape is blunter than a console vendor would like. The APTA's 2020 TKA clinical practice guideline recommends the **KOOS JR, the 30-Second Sit-to-Stand and the Timed Up and Go** — graded "Best Practice" on evidence quality rated "Insufficient" — and contains **no gait-assessment recommendation at all**; gait speed appears only as a research outcome ([APTA TKA CPG](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7462050)). Two of the three recommended measures are not treadmill-measurable. OARSI recommends the **40-metre Fast-Paced Walk Test** for knee OA ([HCPLive](https://www.hcplive.com/view/oarsi-adopts-tests-physical-function-hip-and-knee-oa)), and after ACL reconstruction the dominant clinical metrics are strength and hop-test symmetry rather than gait at all ([Frontiers in Medicine 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2024.1303172/full)). Gait speed earns its place because it is the derived quantity of those recommended walk tests, and because it has usable change thresholds: **0.05 m/s for small meaningful change and 0.10 m/s for substantial change in older adults** ([Perera et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16696738/)), **roughly 0.048–0.115 m/s in radiographic knee OA** ([Gilbert et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7392790)), and a **patient acceptable symptom state of 1.2 m/s after TKA** — from the same study that concluded minimal clinically important improvement for walking speed **is not determinable**, because gait changes were not associated with perceived body function or satisfaction ([CIRRIS record](https://www.cirris.ulaval.ca/publications/walking-speed-and-maximal-knee-flexion-during-gait-after-total-knee-arthroplasty-minimal-clinically-important-improvement-is-not-determinable-patient-acceptable-symptom-state-is-potentially-useful)). Note the tension: the 40mFPWT's own MDC is **0.19 m/s** ([UBC toolkit](https://med-fom-clone-pt.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2024/01/40-metre-Fast-paced-Walk-Test-40mFPWT-.pdf)), nearly four times the "small change" threshold, so single-session point values should never carry a change badge; average across sessions.

Because a treadmill *imposes* speed, the console must distinguish a self-selected-speed protocol — where speed is an outcome comparable to walk-test norms — from a fixed-speed protocol, where speed is an input and the gait variables are the outcome. Mixing them across sessions makes trends uninterpretable. Overground norms (**≈1.43 m/s for men in their 30s–50s falling to ≈0.97/0.94 m/s at 80+**, [Bohannon & Andrews derived tables](https://www.physicaltherapy.utoronto.ca/sites/default/files/assets/files/11walking-speed-age-and-sex-specific-normative-values-final.pdf)) do not strictly apply to treadmill-imposed speeds.

For the rest of the spatiotemporal set, the best-matched reliability data come from 33 healthy adults on an instrumented treadmill, and they set hard floors on what the console can call a change.

Source: [Reed, Urry & Wearing 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3766030/) (Zebris capacitance platform, 120 Hz, 3 sessions; the authors note RC₉₅ is mathematically identical to MDC₉₅, and report **no ICCs**). The same paper summarises between-day MDC₉₅ as **3–17% for temporal, 4–20% for kinetic and 14–33% for spatial parameters** — and notes that session 3 differed significantly from sessions 1–2 on most parameters, a familiarisation effect of the same magnitude as a real clinical effect (cadence +5 steps/min, step length +4 cm, double support −2 %GC). In chronic stroke on a split-belt instrumented treadmill, between-session MDC was **6.75 cm (paretic step length), 5.46 cm (non-paretic), 3.6 %GC (paretic stance) and 4.2 %GC (paretic double support)**, with ICCs 0.895–0.986; within-session MDC was 2–2.5× tighter ([Lewek & Randall 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3042506/)). Two consequences: a stance-phase change must exceed ~2 %GC in healthy treadmill walking or ~3.6 %GC in a pathological population to be real, which is the same magnitude as the entire healthy-versus-pathological difference for some parameters; and a console comparing a patient to themselves within one session can resolve far smaller changes than one comparing across visits, which should be reflected in distinct change-flagging logic.

Symmetry is where the temptation is greatest and the evidence thinnest. Five formulas circulate — ratio index, log-transformed gait asymmetry, symmetry index `SI = (X_NS − X_S)/[0.5(|X_S|+|X_NS|)] × 100`, symmetry angle, and the normalized symmetry index — all scaled so 0 is perfect symmetry, and the paper that lays them out side by side **gives no clinical cut-off for any of them** and is internally inconsistent on whether SI is bounded ([PMC6960348](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6960348/)). Patterson et al. compared four of them in 161 stroke survivors and 81 healthy adults, concluded "there is no accepted standard for assessing symmetry making it difficult to compare across studies and establish criteria to guide clinical decision making", and recommended the symmetry ratio ([McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1644239)) — while Błażkiewicz et al. recommended the symmetry index instead, a direct conflict ([Acta Bioeng Biomech 16(1)](https://actabio.pwr.edu.pl/fcp/DGBUKOQtTKlQhbx08SlkTUARAUWRuHQwFDBoIVURNWHhUFVZpCFghUHcKVj0GQUw/302/public/publikacje/v16-1-2014/4.pdf)). The commonly quoted ±5% band and the **10% "pathological asymmetry" cutoff validated in hip OA** ([PubMed 21722001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21722001)) are single-timepoint classification gates, not change thresholds; drawing a 10% line on a week-to-week trend misuses a diagnostic cutoff. Healthy left–right differences are about **0.8 cm for step and stride length and 0.7% for stance, step, swing and support timing** across 737 children and 82 young adults ([Lythgo et al.](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-14515-5_46)), so a 1–5% displayed asymmetry is within normal human variation and should not render as a finding. Meanwhile the between-session MDC for a step-length symmetry ratio is **0.068** ([Lewek & Randall](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3042506/)) — about 6.8%, landing almost exactly on the 5–10% "asymmetric" band, meaning a measured asymmetry below ~7% is not reliably distinguishable from session-to-session noise. And Wellsandt's bilateral-deficit problem is fatal to symmetry as a headline: **limb symmetry indexes frequently overestimate knee function after ACLR** because the uninvolved limb also weakens ([PubMed 28355978](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28355978/)), so a console showing only symmetry reports "normal" for a bilaterally deconditioned patient. Show the number with its formula printed and no threshold line. Cadence deserves worse: in the Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study, **lower cadence was not significantly associated with incident or worsening knee symptoms, pain with walking, or functional limitations** ([Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Association-of-Walking-Cadence-to-Changes/9984786445502771)) — measurable, reliable, and in this population clinically meaningless.

## Vertical impulse beats peaks; loading rate and flattening indices fail on definition

The vGRF waveform's three landmarks — F1 weight-acceptance peak, F2 mid-stance trough, F3 push-off peak ([Fan et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)) — are robustly measurable, and a defensible design-time band for healthy adults at comfortable overground speed is **F1 ≈ 1.00–1.20 BW, F2 ≈ 0.70–0.80 BW, F3 ≈ 1.05–1.20 BW**, converging Fan et al. (100/75/104 %BW), a THR study's controls (Fz1 1.05 BW, Fz2 1.117 BW, [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)) and an older review (107/105 %BW). One control dataset reports **F1 of 1.52–1.55 BW** ([PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)), irreconcilable with every other source and probably a speed or processing artefact — flagged, not averaged in. **No published normative table of F1/F2/F3 against walking speed exists for healthy adults**, which matters because F1 and F3 rise with speed while F2 falls: peak magnitude alone cannot distinguish a slow healthy walker from a cautious patient, so speed-matching or speed as a covariate is mandatory. Peak reliability is good — between-day MDC₉₅ of **±43 to ±66 N** in healthy adults on an instrumented treadmill ([Reed et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3766030/)), **≈±0.1 BW** in healthy seniors ([Gait & Posture](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966636212001233)), and **±3% walking / ±11% running** in children ([PubMed 31648119](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31648119/)) — converging on a defensible floor of **0.05–0.10 BW between sessions**, with running roughly doubling it. Reliability figures were derived from captures averaging **52 ± 5 steps**, and ~20 strides per limb is the floor used for loading rate; a session summary built on fewer than ~20 steps per limb has no literature support.

Impulse is the better bet, and it is the metric a vertical-only deck computes most natively. Its rationale is that it captures magnitude *and* duration in one number, which makes it more sensitive to the slow, prolonged-stance strategies that dominate post-surgical and arthritic gait ([PMC4101393](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4101393/)). Using stance sub-phase windows of **loading response 0–16%, mid-stance 16–50%, terminal stance 50–83%, pre-swing 83–100%**, the single largest effect in a THR gait study was **loading-response impulse: 0.117 (SE 0.005) on the operated side versus 0.146 (SE 0.004) Ns/BW in controls, p < 0.0001**; the *non-operated* side was equally abnormal in a different sub-phase, with pre-swing impulse 0.114 versus 0.149, p < 0.0001 ([PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)). Knee OA shows a parallel ~23% weight-acceptance impulse deficit (0.113 ± 0.014 versus 0.146 ± 0.010), and in that cohort **total impulse symmetry ratio was the best single discriminator of OA from control at top walking speed: AUC 0.902, cut-off −3%, sensitivity 88%, specificity 95%** ([PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)) — the only diagnostic-accuracy figure attached to any symmetry metric anywhere in these notes, and a strong argument for impulse-based over peak-based symmetry. Four cautions travel with it. **Two incompatible conventions circulate**: gross impulse `∫F dt` (≈0.6–0.7 BW·s per step at comfortable speed) and net impulse `Σ(GRF − mg)·dt`, which integrates to ~0 over a stride; the THR paper states the net formula but reports whole-cycle magnitudes consistent with gross, so its formula and its numbers cannot both be right. The knee OA paper's unit "BWN/s" is almost certainly a typo for BW·s. **Impulse and peak force move in opposite directions in cautious gait** — impulse rises with slow, long stance while peaks fall — so a dashboard showing impulse alone makes a guarding patient look *more* loaded; report it alongside stance time and peak force, or normalise by stance time. And **no MDC for vertical impulse exists in any retrieved source**, so the console must derive and publish its own.

Loading rate should not ship as a headline number. Four non-interchangeable definitions produce values differing by up to **3× on identical data** — maximum slope 20–80% to the impact peak gave 1036.0 N/kg/s at preferred running speed against 603.5 for the initial-contact-to-impact-peak average — and method interacted significantly with speed (p < 0.001), leading the authors to state that thresholds transfer across neither method nor speed ([PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/)). Worse, two of the four definitions need an impact peak that was **absent in 36.0% of trials at 2.68 m/s, 28.1% at preferred speed and 22.4% at 4.47 m/s** — in *running*; walking does not present one reliably at all. Units are reported inconsistently (N/kg/s versus BW/s, a factor of g), **no published MDC for loading rate exists**, and the one walking-specific clinical paper's values of 34–41 %BW/s are dimensionally implausible — F1 ≈ 100 %BW reached in 0.1–0.15 s implies 700–1100 %BW/s ([Fan et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)); do not use them as reference values. If the console shows a loading rate at all, use the impact-peak-free form **F1 / t(F1) in BW/s** with the definition printed on screen, and never without the measured speed and filter cut-off. A waveform "flattening index" such as `F2/((F1+F3)/2)` is tempting — flattening is a real, well-evidenced pathological signature, with both knee OA humps collapsing toward 1.0 BW ([PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/); [arXiv 2510.16942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16942), n = 12 per group) — but **no standard named ratio metric exists, and there are no normative values, no speed regression, no reliability and no MDC for any version of it**. It would be an invented metric. Leave it out, or ship it explicitly labelled as derived and exploratory.

Cumulative load is worth building and worth under-claiming. The best-specified published implementation integrates impulse above thresholds, in BW·hour, using **≥0.3 BW for overall load and ≥3.0 BW for high-level load**, and the decisive finding is that **the thresholded measure outperformed the unthresholded one** against function: high-level load per hour correlated r = 0.687 with double-leg heel-raise height and r = 0.625 with fast isokinetic peak moment, against r ≤ 0.543 for overall load ([PMC12236876](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12236876/) — medRxiv preprint, n = 15). So report cumulative impulse above clinically meaningful thresholds, plus step count, as both a raw total and a rate. But **no consensus definition, no standard units and no validated clinical thresholds or progression rules exist**, and the bone-adaptation literature implies cumulative load should be magnitude-weighted with an exponent rather than linearly summed if the intent is tissue stimulus ([US 10405780](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10405780)) — a linear sum treats 10,000 low-force steps as equivalent to 1,000 high-force ones. Any "bone stimulus" claim needs the exponent and its source.

One clinical use case stands above the rest for this hardware, and it is pre-operative. In 55 ACL-reconstruction completers split purely by whether injured-limb F1 was below or at/above the uninjured limb, the lesser-loading group's knee extensor strength symmetry improved only 67%→75% (p = .11) against 66%→87% (p = .001) in the greater-loading group, with a **between-group difference at one year of p = .03 and Cohen d = 3.39**; cocontraction and knee flexor strength also diverged, while **KOOS showed no between-group differences at either timepoint** ([Fan et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)). A trivially simple binary limb comparison at preferred walking speed predicted a year-out neuromuscular outcome with a very large effect — and it needs nothing a vertical deck cannot measure. The counterweight is that **walking appears too easy a task to expose arthroplasty loading asymmetry**: walking peak-force LSI did not change across a 6-month TKA intervention (PIF LSI 98.8 ± 14.4%, PPF LSI 101.4 ± 11.6%, p = 0.296–0.418, n = 35, [PMC11539413](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539413/)) while sit-to-stand asymmetry persisted beyond one year in meta-analysis (pooled LSI 0.96, 95% CI 0.93–0.99, [PubMed 37774467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37774467/)). The 90–110% LSI abnormality band in common circulation has **no derivation in the paper that states it**, and control asymmetry is not zero — ASI ≈ 9.0 ± 6.9% in healthy adults versus 18.0 ± 14.4% in knee OA ([PMC2948025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2948025/)) — so an individual's single-session LSI of 90% or 110% sits within one SD of a post-TKA group mean. Trend over sessions is the defensible use; a single-session verdict is not.

## Body-weight support flattens the waveform that half these metrics need

The best quantitative source for what unloading does to the vertical waveform is a harness study of 18 healthy adults on embedded force plates at 240 Hz, and its numbers should govern the console's behaviour in offloaded modes.

| BWS | 1st peak (%BW) | Mid-stance valley (%BW) | 2nd peak (%BW) | Weight-acceptance rate (BW/s) | Push-off rate (BW/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 104 (4.65) | 76 (5.87) | 93 (4.33) | 5.78 (1.27) | 4.66 (0.70) |
| 15% | 86 (6.77) | 67 (3.78) | 76 (4.69) | 5.72 (1.90) | 3.20 (0.89) |
| 30% | 73 (7.01) | 60 (4.82) | 66 (4.88) | 5.82 (2.05) | 2.33 (0.52) |

Source: [Barela et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/). Three readings matter. First, **the waveform is not a scaled copy**: peaks fall faster than the valley (first peak −31 points, valley −16 points across 30% offload), so modulation depth collapses and at 30% BWS the trace is effectively unimodal with almost no distinction between peaks and valley — the authors describe this qualitatively from figures without a formal test of hump distinctness. **Expect F1/F2/F3 detection, M-shape stance sub-phase segmentation and any peak-ratio metric to become unreliable somewhere between 15% and 30% offload.** That is a hard constraint on the console: sub-phase impulse windows anchored on waveform landmarks must fall back to time-fraction windows under offload. Second, **weight-acceptance rate did not change with offload at all (p > 0.5) while push-off rate fell roughly in proportion** — so the two rates carry independent information, and harness unloading reduces peak magnitude without reducing the rate of rise at heel strike. If the clinical goal is protecting a healing joint from impact rather than from peak load, harness BWS is much less effective than the peak reduction implies. Third, this finding **directly conflicts** with a 2021 LBPP report that Fmax *and* loading rate fall linearly with AlterG support ([doi:10.1186/s13102-021-00258-4](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-021-00258-4)). The conflict is plausibly a real mechanism difference — LBPP applies a continuous distributed force that can oppose the limb during loading response, while an overhead harness is compliant and effectively force-constant — but it is unresolved, and **no peer-reviewed study publishes the full LBPP vGRF waveform (peak / valley / peak) at defined offload levels**, because the subject is sealed in a chamber and almost all LBPP force data come from insoles.

Spatiotemporal parameters, by contrast, barely move under walking offload, which argues against attributing small changes to the dial. In 27 healthy adults at 1.34 m/s, moving from 100% to 75% BW changed right stance from **62.2 ± 0.2% to 61.7 ± 0.2% of cycle** and left cadence non-significantly (**p = 0.057**); jogging and running, by contrast, showed large dose-dependent effects (jogging right stance 37.7 → 32.4%, ηp² = 0.782) ([Phillips et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11881985/) — note this paper's impact-force values are implausible as newtons and it contains several internal inconsistencies). Deeper offload does move stance measurably — normalised stance time 0.777 → 0.753 → 0.703 at 100/40/20% BW, n = 29 ([PMC8473299](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8473299/), whose Conclusions contradict its own Results on CoP length and width). And the *surface* effect dwarfs the offload effect: in the same subjects at 0/10/20% harness BWS, treadmill stride length was 0.85–0.93 m against 1.18–1.27 m overground, with significant surface × BWS interactions for stance duration and double-limb support ([Barela et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-019-44652-y)). Match treadmill-versus-overground before interpreting offload at all.

The most actionable finding in this whole area is that **the dial lies, and the deck can tell**. Across 31 adults, measured load was **93.15 ± 1.21% of true body weight at the 100% setting, 35.75 ± 2.89% at the 30% setting and 27.67 ± 3.76% at the 20% setting** ([McNeill et al. 2015](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/accuracy-of-unloading-with-the-anti-gravity-treadmill/); note secondary summaries disagree on the sign of the deep-offload error, so verify against the primary). Support-frame height alone shifts delivered support by **+11.2 to −12.9 percentage points at a 60% setting** — a 48.1% to 71.2% span from setup geometry alone (n = 20, [De Heer et al. 2021](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/alterg-anti-gravity-treadmill-accuracy-of-unloading-is-affected-b/); that abstract's stated 24.1% spread does not match its own figures). The mechanism generalises: delivered force is chamber gauge pressure times effective sealed area at the seal plane, so seal height changes both area and line of action, which is why error grows with the setting. Because the error is systematic and reproducible rather than random, it can be calibrated out *if and only if* the machine measures delivered load — and **no published study has validated commanded LBPP offload against measured load during locomotion**, nor compared standing-measured to walking-measured support, nor characterised hysteresis or within-session drift. The deck's load cells measure exactly the quantity the literature lacks. Report **measured delivered load as % of true body weight, per stride**, and never report the dial setting as if it were the delivered load. The flip side: there is no published reference against which to validate that number on an LBPP machine, so validation has to be done in-house against an instrumented force treadmill or known static masses.

Finally, the clinical case for the machine itself is thinner than its marketing. Unloading demonstrably reduces limb load — **GRFs fell 42% at 60% BW and 79% at 20% BW in 15 post-meniscectomy/post-ACLR patients** ([Eastlack et al. 2005](https://sogacot.org/articulos/ejercicio-en-parte-inferior-del-cuerpo-despues-de-cirugia-de-rodilla-ingles/)) — but the only randomised post-surgical comparison found, a 30-patient TKA pilot, found **KOOS, TUG and pain improved in both arms with no significant between-group difference** against land-based gait training ([Am J Orthop report](https://blogs.the-hospitalist.org/content/use-anti-gravity-treadmill-early-postoperative-rehabilitation-after-total-knee-replacement)). The knee OA signal is more encouraging but comes from single-arm pre/post designs (n = 25, improvements in all KOOS subscales and in hamstring and quadriceps strength, no control group, [U Manitoba](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/handle/1993/8580)) with heavy manufacturer promotion. The widely cited claim that anti-gravity treadmills reduce knee forces — "jogging at 4.5 mph with 50% body weight support provides similar vertical knee joint reaction forces as walking at 2.5–3.5 mph full body weight" — reaches these notes **only through the manufacturer's own summary of a manufacturer-sponsored study using an instrumented knee implant in four subjects**, with the primary text unretrievable and no replication, extension or contradiction found ([AlterG Clinical Research Summary](https://alterg.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/D001121-Rev-A_AlterG-Clinical-Research-Summary.pdf); [Scripps release](https://www.scripps.org/news_items/3747-scripps-researchers-use-smart-knee-implant-to-test-anti-gravity-treadmill-for-rehabilitation)). The same document's claim that "gait mechanics are maintained for all levels of weight support and speed" is contradicted by the primary data above. Do not inherit either claim in the console's copy. Two genuinely good ideas do survive: **log the minimum offload at which the patient walks pain-free, session over session** — knee OA patients needed progressively less support over 12 weeks, which makes the offload setting itself an outcome measure — and if speed is normalised, use **Fr = v²/(g·L) with true g**, where Fr ≈ 0.25 marks comfortable walking and Fr ≈ 0.5 the walk–run transition; substituting an effective gravity will push Froude above 0.5 at ordinary walking speeds under deep offload and generate false "should be running" predictions ([Carr & McGee](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2719915/); [Donelan & Kram](https://www.sfu.ca/locomotionlab/assets/dynsimwalk1997.pdf), whose 67% stride-length discrepancy at matched Fr already breaks dynamic similarity for real reduced gravity).

Three cross-cutting rules fall out of the reporting literature rather than the metrics literature. **Put measurement conditions before the data**: the established clinical gait report template places quality sections first "because they affect how the data should be read", links each finding to its supporting evidence, and states the limits of each data set ([Baker/McGinley/Thomason-derived template](https://www.springermedizin.de/resource/supplementary/12887_2020_2315_MOESM1_ESM.pdf/18337870); [NSHCS competency](https://curriculumlibrary.nshcs.org.uk/stp/competency/SPE340c2/8/)). For this console that means belt speed, offload setting *and* measured delivered load, handrail use, step count, and a per-session data-quality indicator distinguishing "this patient changed" from "this measurement changed" — the central argument of the sensing-quality literature, since fixed sensor fusion alters a measurement "without indicating that sensing quality has changed" ([arXiv 2609.22619](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.22619)). **Cite every threshold you draw on screen**, and draw none you cannot cite: FDA clinical decision support guidance expects the basis for a recommendation to be reviewable by the intended clinician, with clinical practice guidelines named as the clearest acceptable basis and published literature accepted ([Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/newsroom/alerts/2022/October/Is-Your-Clinical-Decision-Support-Software-a-Medical-Device)) — which is both a regulatory and an epistemic argument against invented symmetry lines. **Default to aggregates and trends, not streams**: clinicians in participatory dashboard design preferred aggregate values and period trends and warned against excessive visualisation ([JMIR Form Res 2023;7:e46866](https://formative.jmir.org/2023/1/e46866/PDF)), and the documented failure mode of instrumented gait analysis in routine practice is interpretation burden — cost, protocol complexity, and the expertise and time needed to read the output ([PMC12398296](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12398296/); [Frontiers in Medical Technology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medical-technology/articles/10.3389/fmedt.2022.901331/full)) — so adding channels without adding interpretation adds to the known problem.

## Conclusion

The hardware is better than the evidence it would serve. A four-cell vertical deck at 940 Hz can produce a credible per-limb vertical force stream with roughly 2% decomposition error, a millimetre-to-centimetre CoP trajectory, and every impulse and cumulative-load construct in the literature — and the binding limits are not the sensors but the clinical science: no MDC for impulse, loading rate or any symmetry index; no MCID for cadence, step length or stance fraction; no consensus symmetry formula; no normative F1/F2/F3 table against speed; no normative data of any kind for gait at defined offload levels, where the entire retrieved literature rests on cohorts of **n ≤ 31**, mostly healthy young adults, on single devices. The right response is not to wait for that evidence but to invert the product logic: lead with the measurement that is trustworthy *because* it is a measurement rather than an inference — delivered load at the foot, per step, per limb, against a prescription — and treat the gait dashboard as secondary context with its uncertainty on the face of it.

Two asymmetries are worth carrying into the design. The first is that this device's **measurement obligations and its commercial differentiation are the same work**: nobody has published decomposition accuracy below 0.4 m/s, under body-weight support, or with handrail use; nobody has validated commanded against delivered LBPP offload during locomotion; and the industry's commercial standard of evidence for per-limb decomposition is to cite the method papers rather than publish device-level validation ([Arsalis Gaitway 3D](https://www.arsalis.com/products/instrumented-treadmill/)). Characterising the slow, offloaded, handrail-assisted failure envelope would both de-risk the product and be genuinely novel. The second is that the two mechanisms this machine combines pull in opposite directions on the same screen: unloading suppresses peaks without suppressing weight-acceptance rate (under harness support, at least), and simultaneously flattens the waveform until the landmarks those metrics are built on stop existing. A console that reports peak-based metrics and offload on the same page without encoding that interaction will show a clinician a flattening curve and a falling peak, and leave them to guess whether the patient improved, the dial moved, or the algorithm lost the hump.
