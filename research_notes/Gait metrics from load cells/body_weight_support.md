# Gait Measurement on Body-Weight-Support (BWS) and Anti-Gravity / LBPP Treadmills

> **Terminology used throughout.** *Harness BWS* = overhead suspension/counterweight system applying an upward force to a torso-pelvis harness. *LBPP* = lower body positive pressure, a sealed chamber pressurised around the lower body, with the seal at the waist/iliac crest; AlterG (now Lifeward) is the dominant commercial example, derived from NASA differential air pressure (DAP) work. These two are mechanically different and the literature should not be pooled. Where a source is manufacturer-sponsored or manufacturer-published, that is stated explicitly.

---

## Q1. How do spatiotemporal parameters change as body weight support increases?

### Takeaway
Spatiotemporal gait is remarkably robust to unloading during *walking* (changes of only 1–3% in stance fraction and <2% in stride length up to 25–30% offload), but becomes strongly dose-dependent during *jogging and running*, where stance fraction falls, stride length grows and cadence drops roughly linearly with offload. The single most consistent finding across both harness and LBPP systems is a reduction in stance/double-support fraction and an increase in flight/swing fraction.

### Cited Findings

**LBPP, walking + jogging + running (largest healthy-adult spatiotemporal dataset found)**
- n = 27 healthy adults (7 M / 20 F, 22.3 ± 2.1 y, 67.6 ± 9.6 kg) on an LBPP treadmill at 100%, 90% and 75% BW; three speeds: walking 1.34 m/s, jogging 2.24 m/s, running 3.13 m/s; 3-min trials; measured with Digitsole Pro instrumented insoles — [Phillips et al., Int J Exerc Sci 2025 (PMC11881985)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11881985/)
- **Walking (1.34 m/s), 100% → 90% → 75% BW:** cadence 116.4 ± 1.1 → 117.4 ± 1.1 → 113.7 ± 2.0 steps/min (p = 0.057, *not* significant); right stride length 1.38 ± 0.01 → 1.359 ± 0.01 → 1.356 ± 0.01 m (p = 0.018); right stance 62.2 ± 0.2 → 62.2 ± 0.2 → 61.7 ± 0.2 % of cycle (p < 0.001); left stance 62.3 → 61.9 % (p = 0.016). Step clearance unaffected (p = 0.933 / 0.266) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11881985/)
- **Jogging (2.24 m/s):** cadence 159.5 → 157.7 → 152.6 steps/min (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.684); right stride length 1.72 → 1.74 → 1.79 m (p < 0.001); right stance 37.7 → 35.6 → 32.4 % (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.782) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11881985/)
- **Running (3.13 m/s):** cadence 164.7 → 162.3 → 157.0 steps/min (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.835); right stride length 2.31 → 2.35 → 2.42 m (p < 0.001); right stance 32.4 → 31.3 → 29.1 % (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.820) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11881985/)
- Authors' summary: body weight condition significantly affected cadence, stride length, stance time and impact force during running (p < 0.001); walking showed few differences; jogging differed across all BW levels for every variable except GRF — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11881985/)
- **Caveat flagged in the source text:** the paper names "Boost 2 Microgravity Treadmill compression shorts" but does not name the treadmill manufacturer; impact-force values are reported in newtons but are ~1.7–2.1, implausible for running vGRF, so they are almost certainly normalised or mislabelled. The paper also contains internal inconsistencies (walking right-stride post hoc given as both p = 0.004 and p < 0.004; jogging left-impact 90% and 75% means identical at 1.64 ± 0.07; jogging right-impact 90% value missing) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11881985/)

**LBPP, deeper offload (40% and 20% BW)**
- n = 29 healthy college-aged volunteers (24.7 ± 4.0 y; 11 M / 18 F) on an **AlterG** LBPP treadmill at 100%, 40% and 20% BW, self-selected comfortable speed, 3.5 min per condition, Medilogic pressure insoles at 60 Hz. Normalised stance time 0.777 ± 0.071 (100%) → 0.753 ± 0.096 (40%) → 0.703 ± 0.126 (20%); omnibus p < 0.05 and *all three* pairwise comparisons significant, including 40% vs 20% — [Sensors 2021 (PMC8473299)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8473299/)
- Same study: centre-of-pressure path **length** fell (0.626 ± 0.076 → 0.438 ± 0.223 → 0.383 ± 0.190, normalised to insole dimensions) and COP path **width** rose (0.109 ± 0.023 → 0.152 ± 0.037 → 0.162 ± 0.043) with unloading; 100% differed from both reduced conditions (p < 0.05) but 40% vs 20% did not (p = 0.259 length, p = 0.134 width) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8473299/)
- Note: that paper's Conclusions section states COP length *increased* and width *decreased*, the opposite of its own Results and Discussion — an internal contradiction worth flagging if cited — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8473299/)
- The same study explicitly did **not** measure stride length, step length or step width; it *infers* increased stride rate and possibly shorter stride from the stance-time change — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8473299/)

**Harness BWS, treadmill vs overground (direct comparison of surface effect)**
- n = 14 non-disabled young adults (7 M / 7 F, 26 ± 4.4 y, 1.73 ± 0.08 m, 73.4 ± 14.4 kg) at 0%, 10%, 20% BWS, on a treadmill and on an overground rail-cart system, speed set to ~80% of self-selected baseline (baseline 1.28 ± 0.14 m/s; test 1.03 ± 0.10 m/s) — [Barela et al., Sci Rep 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-019-44652-y) (also [PMC6544641](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6544641/))
- **Treadmill, 95% CI by BWS level:** stride length 0.85–0.93 m (0%) → 0.83–0.91 (10%) → 0.82–0.91 (20%); step length 0.43–0.47 → 0.42–0.46 → 0.41–0.46 m; stance 63.9–66.6% → 64.0–66.6% → 63.4–66.1%; double-limb support 27.6–33.1% → 27.8–33.3% → 26.6–32.3% — [same](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-019-44652-y)
- **Overground, same subjects and BWS levels:** stride length 1.18–1.27 → 1.18–1.27 → 1.15–1.24 m; stance 61.6–63.8% → 58.6–62.0% → 58.0–60.7%; double-limb support 21.7–26.2% → 16.9–22.0% → 16.4–20.6% — [same](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-019-44652-y)
- **Statistics:** BWS main effect on stride length F(2,26) = 4.034, p = 0.03; on step length F(2,26) = 3.58, p = 0.04; on stance duration F(2,26) = 23.45, p < 0.01; on double-limb support F(2,26) = 21.10, p < 0.01. **Surface × BWS interactions were significant** for stance duration (F(2,26) = 11.10, p < 0.01) and double-limb support (F(2,26) = 10.50, p < 0.01). Surface main effects were an order of magnitude larger than BWS effects (e.g. stride speed F(1,13) = 1019.75) — [same](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-019-44652-y)
- The same paper's literature review states that in non-disabled adults on a BWS treadmill, cadence and double-limb support duration decrease while single-limb support duration increases — [same](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-019-44652-y)

**Other anchors**
- A 2017 study reported that in healthy people, walking kinematics stayed close to normal even at **50% unloading**, but muscle activation patterns during stance changed to hold speed — [J NeuroEng Rehabil 2017 (DOAJ record)](https://doaj.org/article/ed5d6ff82787463b9a635003a3de5872)
- In a conference abstract, walking at 3.5 km/h under progressive LBPP unloading showed a shift from **rear-foot to fore-foot strike**, ankle angles increasing, and knee and hip ranges decreasing — [Frontiers in Physiology conference abstract 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/10.3389/conf.fphys.2018.26.00024/event_abstract)
- A study of competitive runners on an LBPP treadmill used **nine** body-weight-support levels with pressure insoles and inertial sensors — the densest offload sampling found — [PMC12066903](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12066903/)
- A 2020 LBPP study tested walking at 2.5 km/h and 3.5 km/h with 50%, 25% and 0% BWS — [PubMed 32243378](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32243378/)

### Inferences
- The *surface* (treadmill vs overground) effect on stride length, stance % and double support is far larger than the *offload* effect at clinically typical levels. Any normative or reference comparison must be matched for treadmill vs overground before offload is interpreted; the significant surface × BWS interaction in Barela 2019 means the offload-vs-parameter slope is itself different on a treadmill.
- From the walking data, the clinically relevant dose-response for spatiotemporal parameters in the 0–30% offload band is small: ~0.5–0.6 percentage points of stance fraction per 25% offload (Phillips 2025), and ~1–3 cm of stride length per 20–25% offload. Deeper offload (to 20% BW) does move stance fraction measurably (Sensors 2021). So a console reporting stance/swing at modest offload should expect the signal to be near the noise floor, and should probably not attribute small changes to the offload setting.
- Walking vs running diverge because running stance fraction is set largely by flight time, which scales with the vertical impulse the body must generate; unloading reduces the required impulse, so stance shortens. In walking, double support is set by the inverted-pendulum step transition and is far less sensitive.

### Gaps
- No single study found reports *all* of cadence, step length, stride length, stance %, swing %, single support % and double support % across a fine ladder of offload levels at matched speed in one cohort. The parameters have to be assembled across studies with different devices, speeds and populations.
- Double-support fraction under **LBPP** specifically: I found no study reporting double-support % at defined LBPP offload levels. The double-support data above are from harness systems (Barela 2019).
- The relationship between offload and gait variability/asymmetry metrics (step-time CV, symmetry indices) at defined offload levels was not found in peer-reviewed form.

## Q2. How does the vertical GRF waveform change under BWS and under LBPP specifically? Does the double hump persist, flatten or change?

### Takeaway
Under harness BWS the double hump progressively **flattens**: both peaks and the mid-stance valley all fall, but the peaks fall faster than the valley, so by 30% BWS the M-shape is nearly indistinguishable from a plateau. Crucially, the **weight-acceptance (loading) rate is unchanged by unloading**, while push-off rate falls roughly in proportion — the waveform does not simply scale down.

### Cited Findings

**Harness BWS, overground, full waveform decomposition (the best quantitative source found)**
- n = 18 healthy young adults (9 M / 9 F, 27 ± 4 y), customised overground harness on a suspended rail with servo-controlled speed, 0% / 15% / 30% BWS, two embedded force plates at 240 Hz, 20 Hz filter, normalised to % body weight. Speed 1.44 ± 0.17 m/s without BWS vs 1.16 ± 0.12 m/s with BWS — [Barela et al., Braz J Phys Ther 2014;18(6):572–579 (PMC4311602)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/)
- **Right leg, mean (SD), % BW:**

| BWS | 1st vertical peak | Mid-stance valley | 2nd vertical peak | Weight-acceptance rate (BW/s) | Push-off rate (BW/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 104 (4.65) | 76 (5.87) | 93 (4.33) | 5.78 (1.27) | 4.66 (0.70) |
| 15% | 86 (6.77) | 67 (3.78) | 76 (4.69) | 5.72 (1.90) | 3.20 (0.89) |
| 30% | 73 (7.01) | 60 (4.82) | 66 (4.88) | 5.82 (2.05) | 2.33 (0.52) |

  — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/)
- First peak, valley and second peak all decreased with increasing BWS (p < 0.001). **Weight-acceptance rate did not differ across BWS levels (p > 0.5).** Push-off rate decreased with BWS (p < 0.001) — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/)
- Shape description: at 0% BWS the curve showed two well-defined peaks and a valley; at 30% BWS the curve was flattest, with almost no distinction between the two peaks and the valley; 15% BWS produced "flatter curves" than 0%. The authors did **not** run a formal statistical test of hump distinctness — the shape claim is qualitative/figure-based — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/)
- Left/right symmetry: the only significant side difference was the anterior-posterior deceleration peak (higher on the left, p < 0.005); no vertical GRF variable differed by side — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/)

**LBPP-specific vGRF**
- Grabowski (2010), the foundational LBPP walking study: n = 10 healthy volunteers walked at 1.0, 1.25 and 1.5 m/s under five unloading levels from 25% BW to full BW, with metabolic power, GRFs and stride kinematics measured. Finding: faster walking raised peak GRFs and metabolic demand while weight support lowered both; many velocity × BW combinations produced similar aerobic demands — [Grabowski A., Arch Phys Med Rehabil 2010;91:951–957](https://alterg.com/treadmill-training-rehab/health-and-medical/new-journal-article-highlights-potential-benefits-of-lower-body-positive-pressure-in-orthopedic-rehabilitation) (study summary via an AlterG press page — **manufacturer-hosted**; the primary article was not retrievable in full text)
- A 2005 ISB conference abstract (Li & Cropper) reported that maximum vertical GRF **increases with walking speed and decreases with the amount of body weight supported** on an instrumented BWS treadmill; the retrieved text cut off before full results — [ISB 2005 abstract](https://media.isbweb.org/images/conf/2005/abstracts/0010.pdf)
- A 2021 LBPP paper reports that **Fmax and loading rate decrease linearly** as AlterG-assisted BW support is increased; the fitted slope was not visible in the retrieved excerpt — [BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil 2021, doi:10.1186/s13102-021-00258-4](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-021-00258-4) (this contradicts Barela 2014's harness finding of *unchanged* loading rate — see Inferences)
- In-shoe regional loading on an LBPP treadmill: n = 10 runners, five BW settings × three speeds, significant main effects of both running speed and BW setting on whole-foot peak force and impulse (p < 0.001) — [Record 4035268, IAT database](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4035268?lng=en)
- Elite-athlete LBPP running thesis: tibial shock fell as support rose, but **not linearly**; ankle angle changes were trivial-to-small across all conditions except initial contact and mid-stance at 60% support — [AUT thesis](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/9830)

### Inferences
- The waveform under BWS is **not a scaled copy** of the unsupported waveform. Peaks fall more than the valley (in Barela 2014: 1st peak −30% BW units from 104→73 over a 30% offload, while the valley fell only 76→60, i.e. −16), so peak-to-valley modulation depth collapses faster than mean force. A console that reports "peak vGRF as % BW" will therefore *understate* the preservation of mid-stance load and *overstate* how much the gait pattern has changed, and vice versa for a console reporting mean stance force.
- The unchanged weight-acceptance rate (Barela 2014) is the single most important engineering finding here: harness unloading reduces peak magnitude but not the rate of rise at heel strike, presumably because the suspension force is quasi-static over the ~50–100 ms of loading response while limb-segment deceleration is unaffected. If the goal is to protect a healing joint from *impact* (rate), harness BWS may be much less effective than the peak reduction suggests.
- The apparent conflict between Barela 2014 (loading rate unchanged, harness) and the 2021 AlterG paper (loading rate decreases linearly, LBPP) is plausibly a real mechanism difference rather than a measurement artefact: LBPP applies the lifting force continuously and distributed over the pelvis/thigh seal, so it can oppose the limb during the loading response, whereas an overhead harness is compliant and effectively force-constant. This needs the 2021 full text to confirm and would be a high-value check.
- At 30% BWS the vGRF trace is effectively unimodal, which breaks any gait-event or metric algorithm that relies on detecting two peaks or a mid-stance trough (e.g. peak-ratio metrics, M-shape-based stance sub-phase segmentation). Expect peak-detection to become unreliable somewhere between 15% and 30% offload.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed study was found that publishes the full LBPP vGRF waveform (1st peak / valley / 2nd peak separately) at defined offload levels.** The waveform decomposition data above are from a harness system. This is the single largest gap for the stated objective, and it is a real gap in the literature, not just in my search: LBPP makes force-plate measurement hard because the subject is inside a sealed chamber, so most LBPP force data come from in-shoe insoles (which measure plantar pressure, not true GRF, and miss shear and any force transmitted through the seal).
- No study was found quantifying how the mid-stance valley depth, or a peak-to-valley ratio, varies as a continuous function of offload.
- No study was found reporting vGRF waveform shape under LBPP in post-surgical or arthritic populations.

## Q3. Harness/suspension BWS vs LBPP: effect on kinematics and kinetics — which preserves normal gait better, and by how much?

### Takeaway
I found **no study that directly compares harness BWS with LBPP in the same subjects**, so no quantified "by how much" answer exists in the literature. Indirect comparison favours LBPP for kinematic preservation at matched offload (LBPP studies report non-significant or small kinematic changes up to 30–50% offload; harness studies report significant stance and double-support changes at only 10–20% offload), but the comparison is confounded by device, speed and population.

### Cited Findings
- **The absence is explicit:** a targeted search for a harness-vs-LBPP comparison returned studies of each modality separately, with different populations (healthy walkers, elite athletes, competitive runners), speeds and support levels, which cannot be directly compared — searches run against [Frontiers Sports & Active Living](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2020.614559/pdf), [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/9830) and [JSSM](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-04-583.xml-abst)
- **Mechanism distinction:** LBPP systems use differential air pressure in a chamber to reduce the weight of an individual while walking, up to 100% of original body weight, *instead of* using a body-suspension harness system — [Frontiers Sports & Active Living 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2020.614559/pdf)
- **LBPP side, walking:** n = 8 men, walking with **30% body weight support had no statistically significant effect on joint kinematics** or peak series-elastic-element length — [Frontiers Sports & Active Living 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2020.614559/pdf)
- **LBPP side, hip OA + controls:** at 50% BW, peak hip flexion in swing fell from 22.0° to 16.2° (OA) and 25.6° to 21.0° (control), peak knee flexion 47.2° → 39.3° (OA) and 59.9° → 51.9° (control), peak ankle plantarflexion *increased* 5.5° → 11.1° (OA) and 7.0° → 14.6° (control), all BW main effects p < 0.001; **no group × BW interaction for any joint angle**, and the authors concluded gait kinematics were "not drastically altered" relative to controls — [BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2021 (PMC7792168)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7792168/)
- Note the effect sizes in that study are small relative to the measurement system's own minimal detectable change: knee-flexion MDC 7.0°, hip-flexion MDC 5.1°, ankle plantarflexion MDC 5.2°. The 50%-BW hip flexion change (−5.8° OA, −4.6° control) and knee flexion change (−7.9° OA, −8.0° control) sit at or barely above MDC — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7792168/)
- **Harness side:** at only 10–20% BWS, significant main effects on stance duration (p < 0.01) and double-limb support (p < 0.01), plus significant surface × BWS interactions — [Barela et al., Sci Rep 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-019-44652-y)
- **Harness side, kinetics:** harness BWS at 15% and 30% changed peak vGRF substantially while leaving weight-acceptance rate unchanged (see Q2) — [Barela et al., Braz J Phys Ther 2014 (PMC4311602)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/)
- **Harness running:** a Journal of Sports Science and Medicine study compared full weight support with 20% and 40% harness BWS in ten male runners, measuring cycle length, cycle frequency, contact time, joint ROM and vertical COM displacement; findings were not retrievable from the abstract page — [JSSM](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-04-583.xml-abst)
- **Manufacturer claim:** AlterG's own clinical research summary states that "EMG activity shows muscle firing patterns and gait mechanics are maintained, for all levels of weight support and speed" — [AlterG Clinical Research Summary, D001121 Rev A, © 2025 Lifeward Inc.](https://alterg.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/D001121-Rev-A_AlterG-Clinical-Research-Summary.pdf) (**manufacturer document; this is a strong universal claim ("all levels") that the peer-reviewed data above do not support** — Phillips 2025 found significant stance, cadence and stride changes at 75% BW during jogging and running, and the hip OA study found p < 0.001 BW effects on hip, knee and ankle peak angles at 75% and 50% BW)

### Inferences
- A mechanistically grounded reason to expect LBPP to perturb kinematics less: the harness applies a single upward force at the pelvis/torso through a compliant strap, which both adds torso constraint and can produce a vertical bobbing resonance; LBPP applies a distributed pressure field around the whole lower body with the resultant acting near the seal at the iliac crest, leaving the torso and arms unconstrained. Conversely, LBPP introduces its own artefact: the pressure field acts on the *thighs and shanks as they move*, so the delivered vertical force is coupled to limb position within the stride cycle in a way a harness force is not.
- The harness literature's larger apparent effects at smaller offloads may partly reflect that harness studies more often measure spatiotemporal variables (which are sensitive) while LBPP studies more often measure joint angles with wearables of modest resolution (which, per the MDC values above, are insensitive). Any claim that "LBPP preserves gait better" should be treated as unproven rather than established.
- The AlterG "gait mechanics are maintained at all levels" claim should be reported with its provenance and contrasted against the primary data; it is the kind of claim a console vendor may be tempted to inherit.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head harness-vs-LBPP study in the same subjects at matched offload. This is a genuine, reportable gap.
- No study found quantifying the within-stride variation of the delivered LBPP vertical force (see Q4/Q5 — this is the same gap seen from the hardware side).
- No systematic review found that pools harness and LBPP separately with effect sizes per offload level.

## Q4. How accurately does the commanded offload match the actual reduction in GRF? Evidence of discrepancy, hysteresis, or drift?

### Takeaway
There is solid published evidence that commanded and delivered unloading diverge on the AlterG, and the error is **non-monotonic in the setting and strongly dependent on how the user is positioned in the chamber**. At full BW the machine under-delivers load (~93% of true BW); at deep offload (30% and 20% settings) it over-delivers support by ~6–8 percentage points; and support-frame height alone shifts delivered support by **up to ±12 percentage points at a 60% setting** — a 23–24 point spread between extreme frame positions. No published study was found on hysteresis or drift.

### Cited Findings

**Setting accuracy across the range (n = 31)**
- McNeill DKP, De Heer HD, Bounds RG, Coast JR (2015), *J Strength Cond Res* 29(3):863–868, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000000678. n = 31 adults (15 M / 16 F), mean age 29.3 ± 10.9 y, mean body weight 66.55 ± 12.68 kg. Participants were weighed outside the machine, then inside it at BWS levels from 100% to 20% in 10% steps, and predicted (displayed) body weight was compared with measured — [NAU Experts record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/accuracy-of-unloading-with-the-anti-gravity-treadmill/)
- **Results (measured as % of true body weight):** at 100% setting, measured 93.15% ± 1.21 (significantly *lower* than displayed, p < 0.001); 90% through 70% settings, no significant difference; 60% through 40% settings, significant differences (values not available on the retrieved page); 30% setting, measured 35.75% ± 2.89 (p < 0.001, i.e. *more* load than displayed — less support than displayed); 20% setting, measured 27.67% ± 3.76 (p < 0.001). Differences were under 5% except at the extremes — [same](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/accuracy-of-unloading-with-the-anti-gravity-treadmill/)
  - Direction note: at the 30% and 20% *settings* the measured load was *higher* than commanded (35.75% vs 30%; 27.67% vs 20%), i.e. the machine delivered **less** unloading than displayed. (Some secondary summaries state the opposite — "more support than displayed" — so the sign should be checked against the primary article before publication.)

**Support frame height as an error source (n = 20)**
- De Heer HD, Kaufman A, Repka CP, Rojas K, Charley B, Bounds R (2021), *J Strength Cond Res* 35(10):2910–2914, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003223, Northern Arizona University. n = 20 ("10 men/women"), weighed at 10% increments from 0 to 60% BWS with the support frame at hip height (iliac crest, "neutral") and up to 3 positions above or below neutral on a 1–9 numbered frame — [NAU Experts record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/alterg-anti-gravity-treadmill-accuracy-of-unloading-is-affected-b/)
- **At a 10% displayed BWS setting:** 13.1% actual with frame 3 positions higher (+3.0 pts); 5.3% actual with frame 3 positions lower (−4.7 pts). **At a 60% displayed BWS setting:** 71.2% actual with frame 3 positions higher (+11.2 pts); 48.1% actual 3 positions lower (−12.9 pts). Total reported spread at 60% BWS: 24.1% — [same](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/alterg-anti-gravity-treadmill-accuracy-of-unloading-is-affected-b/)
- Deviations grew as BWS increased. Conclusion: frame position significantly affects the AlterG's accuracy in providing BWS; above hip height increases support, below decreases it — [same](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/alterg-anti-gravity-treadmill-accuracy-of-unloading-is-affected-b/)
- Arithmetic inconsistency in that abstract: 71.2 − 48.1 = 23.1, not the stated 24.1; the 24.1 figure is likely the sum of the two deviations (11.2 + 12.9) — flag if citing — [same](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/alterg-anti-gravity-treadmill-accuracy-of-unloading-is-affected-b/)

**Manufacturer position**
- AlterG describes its DAP technology as unweighting the user "precisely and consistently … in 1% increments of the user's actual body weight throughout the range of activities performed within each session" and claims "No other unweighting technology offers as much precision, accuracy and comfort" — [AlterG Clinical Research Summary, D001121 Rev A](https://alterg.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/D001121-Rev-A_AlterG-Clinical-Research-Summary.pdf) (**manufacturer document**)
- AlterG's service FAQ references a "Scale Offset" error that can occur on new installation and directs users to "Base Weight setting instructions" — [AlterG Service FAQ](https://alterg.com/service-faq). These are the only manufacturer calibration references found, and they do not address the published accuracy findings.
- I found **no manufacturer response** to either accuracy study and no published recalibration procedure aimed at the frame-height error.

**Indirect corroboration from force measurement**
- In the Phillips 2025 LBPP study, running impact force fell only ~9.6% (left: 2.09 → 1.89) and ~10.8% (right: 2.13 → 1.90) when the setting was moved from 100% to 75% BW — far less than the commanded 25% offload. However, that study did **not** validate the commanded percentage against measured load, and the force units are suspect (see Q2 caveat), so this is suggestive only — [PMC11881985](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11881985/)
- The Barela 2014 **harness** data show the same pattern more cleanly: at a 15% BWS setting the first vertical peak fell from 104% to 86% BW (−18 points of peak, not −15), and at 30% BWS to 73% (−31 points). Peak vGRF reduction and commanded offload are the *same order* but not equal, and in that dataset the peak fell slightly *more* than commanded — [PMC4311602](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/)

### Inferences
- The frame-height finding is mechanistically important and generalises beyond AlterG: in an LBPP chamber, the delivered vertical force is (chamber gauge pressure) × (effective sealed cross-sectional area at the seal plane), so the *height of the seal relative to the subject's pelvis* changes the effective area and the line of action. Any system that calibrates pressure-to-force from a standing tare and then assumes the relationship holds will carry an error that scales with pressure — which is exactly the pattern observed (error grows with BWS setting).
- Combining the two studies: at a nominal 60% BWS setting the *delivered* support could plausibly span roughly 48–71% depending on setup alone, before any within-stride dynamics are considered. A console that reports gait kinetics "at 40% body weight" based on the dial setting is therefore reporting a number with ~±12 percentage points of setup-dependent uncertainty. Measuring the actual load (e.g. with the treadmill's own load cells) is the only way to close this.
- Because the error depends on frame height and on setting, it is a **systematic, reproducible** error rather than random noise — which means it can in principle be calibrated out per-session if the machine measures delivered load, and cannot be averaged out if it does not.

### Gaps
- **Hysteresis:** no published study found on whether delivered unloading differs when a setting is approached from above vs below (pressurising vs depressurising). This is an obvious question for a pneumatic system with a compliant seal, and the literature appears silent.
- **Drift:** no published study found on drift of delivered unloading within a session (e.g. as the subject sweats, the seal relaxes, chamber temperature rises, or the subject shifts fore-aft on the belt).
- The 60%–40% setting values from McNeill 2015 were not available on the retrieved page; only the significance of the differences. Getting the primary article's tables would complete the accuracy curve.
- No equivalent accuracy study found for **harness** BWS systems (whether displayed/commanded unloading force matches delivered force), nor for non-AlterG LBPP devices.

## Q5. Measuring and verifying actual body weight support *during walking* rather than during quiet standing

### Takeaway
Both published AlterG accuracy studies appear to have measured by **weighing the subject inside the chamber**, which is a quasi-static/standing measurement, and neither abstract states that measurement was made during walking. I found **no study that validated commanded LBPP offload against measured load during locomotion**, nor any that compared standing-measured to walking-measured delivered support. This is a clear and reportable gap.

### Cited Findings
- McNeill 2015 method as published on the retrieved record: participants "were weighed outside the machine, then inside it at body-weight support levels from 100% to 20% in 10% steps." The record **does not state** whether measurement was standing or during walking; it only notes that the differences "may relate to changes in metabolic demand and maximum speed during walking or running" — [NAU Experts record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/accuracy-of-unloading-with-the-anti-gravity-treadmill/)
- De Heer 2021 method: "subjects were weighed at 10% increments from 0 to 60% BWS" at each frame position. The abstract **does not state** whether weighing was standing or during walking/running — [NAU Experts record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/alterg-anti-gravity-treadmill-accuracy-of-unloading-is-affected-b/)
- The dominant measurement approach for load during LBPP locomotion is **in-shoe pressure insoles**, because the subject is sealed inside the chamber and cannot be placed on an external force plate:
  - Medilogic insoles, 60 Hz, 93–162 sensors/insole (1.50 × 0.75 cm each), raw output 0–255 bits with 255 bits = 64 N/cm²; "vertical ground reaction force is computed by summing sensor pressures"; a 40 N threshold defines heel strike and toe-off — [Sensors 2021 (PMC8473299)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8473299/). That paper reported COP and stance-time outcomes and **reported no plantar force or GRF values in its results**, and did not verify the actual delivered load at each setting.
  - Digitsole Pro insoles used for cadence, stride length, stance and "impact force" in [Phillips et al. 2025 (PMC11881985)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11881985/) — with the unit-plausibility problem noted above.
  - Pressure-distribution insoles used for whole-foot peak force and impulse across five BW settings × three speeds in n = 10 runners — [Record 4035268](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4035268?lng=en)
  - Pressure insoles plus inertial sensors across nine BWS levels in n = 26 competitive runners — [PMC12066903](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12066903/)
- Plantar forces "are consistently shown to decrease with unloading across several studies" — [PMC12066903](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12066903/)
- A targeted search for a standing-vs-walking comparison of insole-measured loading on an LBPP treadmill returned no such source.
- Instrumented-treadmill measurement *is* feasible for harness BWS: the 2005 ISB abstract used an instrumented treadmill to measure vertical external forces across several speeds and support levels under a body weight support system — [ISB 2005 abstract](https://media.isbweb.org/images/conf/2005/abstracts/0010.pdf); and Barela 2014 used embedded force plates at 240 Hz for overground harness BWS — [PMC4311602](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4311602/)
- Related validation work exists on biofeedback systems for measuring lower-limb loading "in the dynamic situation" — [Sensors 2017;17(3):658](https://www2.mdpi.com/1424-8220/17/3/658) (not LBPP-specific; relevance to this question is indirect and unverified from the retrieved record)

### Inferences
- The measurement gap and the mechanism gap are the same gap. A standing tare measures (pressure × effective sealed area) with the subject's pelvis in one fixed posture. During walking the trunk translates vertically by ~2–4 cm, the thighs sweep through ~40°, and the chamber volume and seal geometry change within every stride; there is no published basis for assuming the standing-derived support force is the support force delivered during stance. Insole measurement sidesteps the question by measuring foot load directly, but insoles measure plantar *pressure* summed over discrete sensors, miss shear, miss any load path outside the insole footprint, and in the retrieved studies were generally not themselves validated against a force plate under LBPP conditions.
- Practically, for a console with treadmill-deck load cells: the load cells measure the *delivered* vertical load at the foot-belt interface during locomotion, which is precisely the quantity the literature lacks. That means (a) the console can report something the published literature cannot, and (b) there is no published reference against which to validate it on an LBPP machine, so validation would have to be done in-house against an instrumented force treadmill or known static masses.
- Reporting "actual % body weight delivered, measured per stride" is a defensible metric and is arguably *more* trustworthy than the commanded setting, given Q4. Reporting it as agreeing with the setting would not be defensible.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed validation of commanded vs delivered LBPP unloading during walking or running. (Searches for insole-measured standing-vs-walking comparison returned nothing.)
- Neither accuracy study's abstract states the posture of measurement, so even the "standing" characterisation above is an inference from the phrase "were weighed"; the primary articles are needed to confirm.
- No published data on within-stride variation of the LBPP lifting force.
- No published comparison of insole-derived vGRF against force-plate vGRF under LBPP conditions.

### Takeaway
There are **no normative or reference datasets** for anti-gravity treadmill gait in the sense a clinician would use normative data (percentiles by age/sex at defined speed and offload). What exists is a scatter of small, mostly healthy-young-adult studies, each with its own device, speed set, offload ladder and measurement modality. The closest things to reference tables are the four studies tabulated below, all n ≤ 31.

### Inferences
- The field's offload ladders cluster at 100/90/75%, 100/75/50%, 100/40/20% and 0/10/15/20/30% BWS — there is no consensus ladder, which is itself part of why no normative dataset exists. If a console wants comparable numbers, 100 / 75 / 50 / 25% BW is the best-supported set for walking (matched by the hip OA study and bracketed by Phillips 2025 and Grabowski 2010).
- Because stance fraction and double-support fraction are far more sensitive to treadmill-vs-overground and to walking speed than to offload (Q1), any reference values must be reported *per speed*, and offload-only normative bands would be misleading.
- Sample sizes of 8–31 with mostly healthy young adults mean that between-study differences are as likely to reflect cohort and device as offload. Reporting pooled "normal ranges" across these studies would not be defensible.

### Gaps
- No age- or sex-stratified normative data at any offload level.
- No published reference data for older adults or post-surgical patients at defined offload levels beyond the single hip OA study.
- Grabowski 2010's actual GRF and stride tables (the one study with five offload levels × three walking speeds in a single healthy cohort — the closest existing thing to a reference grid for LBPP walking) could not be retrieved in full text; only the manufacturer's summary of it was available. **Obtaining Arch Phys Med Rehabil 2010;91:951–957 directly is the highest-value single follow-up for this question.**

## Q7. Clinical evidence for BWS treadmill training in post-surgical rehabilitation (ACL, TKA, THA, cartilage repair) and in osteoarthritis; outcome measures used

### Takeaway
The clinical evidence is thin and dominated by small single-arm studies and underpowered pilots. The best-designed post-surgical study found — a randomised TKA pilot (n = 30) — found **no difference** between anti-gravity and land-based gait training. The strongest signal is in knee OA, where single-arm LBPP exercise programmes report improved pain, function and thigh strength. Outcome measures are overwhelmingly KOOS, NRS/VAS pain, TUG, thigh muscle strength and walking speed/distance — not gait kinetics.

### Cited Findings

**Post-surgical knee — the foundational feasibility study**
- Eastlack RK, Hargens AR, Groppo ER, Steinbach GC, White KK, Pedowitz RA. "Lower body positive-pressure exercise after knee surgery." *Clin Orthop Relat Res* 2005 Feb;(431):213–219. n = 15 patients (9 after arthroscopic meniscectomy, remainder after ACL reconstruction), walking in a pressurised chamber at normal BW, 60% BW and 20% BW — [abstract reprint](https://sogacot.org/articulos/ejercicio-en-parte-inferior-del-cuerpo-despues-de-cirugia-de-rodilla-ingles/)
- **Result:** "ground reaction forces for surgically treated and contralateral extremities were reduced 42% and 79% from normal body weight conditions at 60% and 20% body weight." Knee ROM decreased only at the lightest load (20% BW) after meniscectomy; pain relief was significant after ACL reconstruction; the authors concluded LBPP exercise is effective at reducing GRFs while safely facilitating gait postoperatively — [same](https://sogacot.org/articulos/ejercicio-en-parte-inferior-del-cuerpo-despues-de-cirugia-de-rodilla-ingles/)
- A later report describes Eastlack-type protocols as walking on the device once weekly for 6 weeks after surgery, with significant pain reduction and improving knee ROM under body-weight support in the early weeks (secondary characterisation; not verified against the primary article).

**TKA — the only randomised trial found**
- Pilot RCT, Bugbee and colleagues: 30 patients randomised to land-based or anti-gravity gait training over 4 weeks of physical therapy. All patients completed rehabilitation protocols without adverse events. **KOOS, TUG and pain (NRS) improved in both groups with no significant between-group differences.** Authors concluded further studies are needed to determine the efficacy of anti-gravity compared with traditional land-based gait training — [The Hospitalist / Am J Orthop report](https://blogs.the-hospitalist.org/content/use-anti-gravity-treadmill-early-postoperative-rehabilitation-after-total-knee-replacement); [full text PDF](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/issues/articles/content_59ffc50fc08afae6b6_ajo04504e167.PDF)
- The same pilot reported that an LBPP treadmill "is associated with minimal cardiovascular effect while producing a significant decrease in ground reaction forces without altering gait kinematics," and listed assistive devices, hydrotherapy and LBPP chambers as modalities that can reduce knee forces during weight-bearing exercise — [same](https://blogs.the-hospitalist.org/content/use-anti-gravity-treadmill-early-postoperative-rehabilitation-after-total-knee-replacement)

**TKA — physiological response**
- Webber S, Horvey K, Yurach M, Butcher S. "Cardiovascular responses in older adults with total knee arthroplasty at rest and with exercise on a positive pressure treadmill." *Eur J Appl Physiol* 2014;114:653–662. Older adults with TKA showed lower heart rate, systolic blood pressure, oxygen consumption and minute ventilation when walking under LBPP; the device enabled TKA patients to walk faster and/or tolerate greater incline — as summarised in [AlterG Clinical Research Summary](https://alterg.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/D001121-Rev-A_AlterG-Clinical-Research-Summary.pdf) (**manufacturer-compiled summary of a peer-reviewed study**)

**Knee OA**
- Peeler J, Christian M, Cooper J, Leiter J, MacDonald P. "Managing Knee Osteoarthritis: The Effects of Body Weight Supported Physical Activity on Joint Pain, Function, and Thigh Muscle Strength." *Clin J Sport Med* 2015 Nov;25(6):518–523. Reported finding: "Participants required dramatically less LBPP support to walk pain free on the treadmill over time"; an LBPP-supported low-load exercise regimen can significantly diminish knee pain, enhance joint function and increase thigh muscle strength while safely promoting pain-free walking exercise in overweight patients with knee OA — as summarised in [AlterG Clinical Research Summary](https://alterg.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/D001121-Rev-A_AlterG-Clinical-Research-Summary.pdf) (**manufacturer-compiled**)
- The underlying University of Manitoba work: 25 obese older adults with knee OA, anti-gravity treadmill walking twice weekly for 12 weeks at the body-weight percentage that minimised pain. Improvements in **all KOOS subscales** and in hamstring and quadriceps strength; **no** significant differences in cardiovascular fitness, SF-12, or average daily pedometer readings. Pre/post measures only, no control group described — [University of Manitoba MSpace record](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/handle/1993/8580)
- Peeler J, Leiter J, MacDonald P. "Effect of Body Weight-Supported Exercise on Symptoms of Knee Osteoarthritis: A Follow-up Investigation." *Clin J Sport Med* 2018. Improvements in knee pain, joint function and thigh muscle strength associated with a 12-week LBPP-supported low-load exercise regime were **maintained well after cessation** of the programme — as summarised in [AlterG Clinical Research Summary](https://alterg.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/D001121-Rev-A_AlterG-Clinical-Research-Summary.pdf) (**manufacturer-compiled**)
- Kawae T, Mikami Y, Fukuhara K, Kimura H, Adachi N. "Anti-gravity treadmill can promote aerobic exercise for lower limb osteoarthritis patients." *J Phys Ther Sci* 2017;29:1444–1448. Pain after walking was significantly greater with level-ground walking than on the anti-gravity treadmill; subjects tolerated faster walking speeds on the anti-gravity treadmill than overground — as summarised in [AlterG Clinical Research Summary](https://alterg.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/D001121-Rev-A_AlterG-Clinical-Research-Summary.pdf) (**manufacturer-compiled**)
- A case series of 22 participants found that a **mean LBPP of 12.4% of body weight** provided significant pain relief during walking (single session) — reported in search results from the knee OA literature; the likely primary source is a *Clinical Interventions in Aging* 2013 article — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/bba1722af1a14ff48526105985865511) (**low confidence: I did not open the primary article to confirm the 12.4% figure or the authorship**)
- A 2021 RCT compared LBPP treadmill walking with conventional therapy in mild-to-moderate knee OA; **18 patients** recruited. Outcomes were not retrievable from the snippet (**low confidence: the record was not opened and could not be distinguished from the hip OA study with a similar identifier**)

**Hip OA / THA**
- The hip OA LBPP study (n = 15 awaiting unilateral THA + 15 controls) found pain on a 0–10 NRS was lower at 50% BW than at 100% BW (p = 0.002) and than at 75% BW (p = 0.018), and lower at 75% than at 100% BW (p = 0.026); numeric pain values appear only in a figure. The authors suggest clinicians weigh the pain benefit more heavily than the kinematic changes, and that **75% BW may be a useful setting for hip OA patients** — [PMC7792168](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7792168/)

**Cartilage repair / meniscal and chondral injury**
- A systematic review of meniscal and chondral knee injuries involving anti-gravity treadmill rehabilitation found "only a limited number of moderate-quality studies" — surfaced in search results; the specific review was not opened (**low confidence on the exact wording and the review's identity**)

**ACL**
- Wilk KE, Macrina LC, Cain L, Dugas JR, Andrews JR. "Recent advances in the rehabilitation of anterior cruciate ligament injuries." *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 2012;42(3):153–171. Cited in the AlterG summary for the point that a majority of individuals who sustain an ACL injury also sustain a bone bruise to the lateral femoral condyle and lateral tibial plateau (i.e. a rationale for unloaded early gait) — [AlterG Clinical Research Summary](https://alterg.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/D001121-Rev-A_AlterG-Clinical-Research-Summary.pdf) (**manufacturer-compiled**)
- A registered trial exists: "Postoperative Rehabilitation After Knee Arthroplasty: Anti-Gravity Treadmill" — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03904030](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03904030) (registration only; results not reviewed)
- **No randomised trial of LBPP treadmill rehabilitation after ACL reconstruction was found.** The closest is the Eastlack 2005 feasibility study (n = 15, not randomised).

**Manufacturer framing of the clinical evidence (for provenance)**
- AlterG's own summary characterises its orthopaedic evidence as: "Progressive loading protocols enable modulation of joint loading forces during closed chain kinetic exercises following orthopedic injury, resulting in decreased pain to lower extremities, improved joint function, increased muscle strength, and reduced muscle atrophy" (refs 12–15), and its geriatric evidence as improvements in knee pain, joint function and thigh muscle strength plus lower heart rate, systolic BP and VO2 (refs 24–26) — [AlterG Clinical Research Summary, D001121 Rev A, © 2025 Lifeward Inc.](https://alterg.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/D001121-Rev-A_AlterG-Clinical-Research-Summary.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Outcome measures actually used** in this literature, in rough order of frequency: KOOS (and subscales), NRS/VAS pain, TUG, isometric/isokinetic thigh muscle strength (quadriceps and hamstrings), self-selected and tolerated walking speed, walking distance, SF-12, pedometer step counts, HR/BP/VO2/VE, knee ROM. Gait *kinetics* (GRF peaks, loading rate) appear almost exclusively as mechanism/feasibility measures in acute single-session studies, not as trial endpoints. A console offering gait-kinetic metrics is therefore offering something the trial literature has not validated as an outcome.
- The TKA pilot's null result against land-based training is the most important clinical finding to report honestly: the mechanistic case for unloading (GRF reduction is real and large) has not translated into demonstrated superiority on patient-reported or functional outcomes in the one randomised comparison found. The reasonable clinical claim is *tolerability and earlier/pain-free loading*, not *better outcomes*.
- The knee OA signal is more encouraging but comes from single-arm pre/post designs (n = 25) and a follow-up study by the same group, with heavy manufacturer promotion of those results. The dose-finding observation — that patients needed progressively *less* support to walk pain-free over 12 weeks — is clinically meaningful and implies the offload setting itself can serve as an outcome measure, which is a genuinely useful idea for a console (log the minimum offload at which the patient walks pain-free, session over session).

### Gaps
- No randomised trial of LBPP/anti-gravity treadmill rehabilitation after ACL reconstruction, THA, or cartilage repair was found. For cartilage repair specifically I found only an indirect reference to a systematic review reporting limited moderate-quality evidence.
- No systematic review or meta-analysis of BWS/anti-gravity treadmill training in *post-surgical orthopaedic* populations was retrieved in full text (the BWSTT systematic-review literature I encountered is dominated by neurological populations — stroke, Parkinson's, cerebral palsy — which are out of scope here).
- Several knee OA figures above (Peeler 2015/2018, Kawae 2017, Webber 2014) are cited here **only via the manufacturer's compiled summary**; the primary articles were not retrievable and the effect sizes, sample sizes and confidence intervals are therefore unknown. These should be obtained before any of their numbers are reported as findings.
- The "mean LBPP of 12.4% BW for pain-free walking" figure and the n = 18 knee OA RCT are low-confidence leads, not verified findings.

## Q8. Knee / hip joint loading as a function of treadmill speed and offload level — Patil et al. 2013 and successors

### Takeaway
Patil et al. 2013 exists and is the key citation, but I could **not retrieve its abstract or any of its primary numbers** — PubMed, Europe PMC and Wiley all failed (Europe PMC returned hitCount 0 for the title and for the author+topic query; Wiley returned HTTP 403). The only substantive statement I could source is the **manufacturer's own one-sentence summary**. ### Cited Findings

**The citation itself (two conflicting forms)**
- As the user stated and as commonly cited: Patil S, Steklov N, Bugbee WD, Goldberg T, Colwell CW Jr, D'Lima DD. "Anti-gravity treadmills are effective in reducing knee forces." *J Orthop Res* 2013;31(5):672–679 — citation surfaced in search results, with the note that the full text was not available.

- Grabowski 2010 (n = 10 healthy, LBPP, 1.0/1.25/1.5 m/s × five unloading levels 25–100% BW): faster walking raised peak GRFs and metabolic demand; weight support lowered both; many velocity × BW combinations produced similar aerobic demands — [Arch Phys Med Rehabil 2010;91:951–957, as summarised by AlterG](https://alterg.com/treadmill-training-rehab/health-and-medical/new-journal-article-highlights-potential-benefits-of-lower-body-positive-pressure-in-orthopedic-rehabilitation) (**manufacturer-hosted summary**). The same group's earlier running work identified "velocity and weight support combinations that resulted in different peak GRFs, yet demanded the same metabolic power" — [same](https://alterg.com/treadmill-training-rehab/health-and-medical/new-journal-article-highlights-potential-benefits-of-lower-body-positive-pressure-in-orthopedic-rehabilitation)
- Eastlack 2005 provides the only *patient* limb-load numbers at defined LBPP settings: GRFs reduced 42% at 60% BW and 79% at 20% BW relative to normal BW, in 15 post-meniscectomy/post-ACLR patients — [abstract reprint](https://sogacot.org/articulos/ejercicio-en-parte-inferior-del-cuerpo-despues-de-cirugia-de-rodilla-ingles/)
- In-shoe regional loading on an LBPP treadmill showed significant main effects of **both** running speed and BW setting on whole-foot peak force and impulse (p < 0.001), n = 10 runners, five BW settings × three speeds — [Record 4035268](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4035268?lng=en)
- Hip OA: unweighting to 50% BW reduced pain significantly but altered peak hip and knee flexion by amounts at or barely above the measurement system's MDC; the authors recommended 75% BW as a practical setting — [PMC7792168](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7792168/)

### Inferences
- The Patil claim as stated is an **iso-load equivalence**: jogging at 4.5 mph with 50% BWS ≈ walking at 2.5–3.5 mph at full BW, in vertical knee joint reaction force. That is a genuinely useful clinical dosing statement, and it implies the speed effect on tibiofemoral force is large enough that a ~50% offload only buys back the load difference between slow walking and jogging. Note the wide equivalence band (2.5–3.5 mph is a 40% speed range), which suggests the underlying relationship was noisy or the equivalence was approximate.
- With n = 4 instrumented subjects, all of whom are TKA patients with a telemetric tibial tray, the study's external validity to intact knees (ACL, cartilage repair, early OA) is weak: a replaced knee has different geometry, different soft-tissue constraint and different muscle activation than a native knee. Reporting Patil's conclusions as applying to native knees would be an overreach.
- The combination of manufacturer sponsorship, n = 4, and an unretrievable primary text means the "Anti-gravity treadmills are effective in reducing knee forces" headline should be reported in the final write-up with all three caveats attached. The directional claim (knee force falls with offload and rises with speed) is independently corroborated by GRF and in-shoe data; the specific iso-load equivalence is not.
- Mechanistically, tibiofemoral force is dominated by muscle co-contraction rather than by body weight alone, which is why offload does not reduce joint force proportionally and why speed matters so much. That is also why GRF-based consoles cannot be used to infer joint force without a model.

### Gaps
- **Patil et al. 2013's primary numbers are not in hand.** Specifically missing: the exact n, the speeds and offload levels tested, tibiofemoral force magnitudes in body-weight units, and the correlation/regression coefficients behind "strong correlation." PubMed (cookie wall), Europe PMC (hitCount 0 for both title and author queries — the article appears not to be indexed there under that title), and Wiley (HTTP 403) all failed. **Retrieving doi:10.1002/jor.22272 / the J Orthop Res 31(5):672–679 article is the top priority follow-up for this question.** The Europe PMC zero-hit result also raises a small possibility that the exact title or year differs from the commonly cited form and should be verified.
- **No replication, extension or contradiction found.** I found no second instrumented-implant study of anti-gravity treadmill knee loading, and no musculoskeletal-modelling study that estimates knee or hip contact force as a function of LBPP offload and speed. If none exists, that absence is itself the finding: the central mechanistic claim behind anti-gravity treadmill orthopaedic rehab rests on one small manufacturer-sponsored study.
- No study found on **hip** joint contact force as a function of offload and speed (the instrumented-implant hip literature exists — Bergmann and colleagues — but I found no LBPP application of it).
- No study found on patellofemoral loading under LBPP, which matters for cartilage repair and anterior knee pain.

## Q9. Froude number: definition, use for normalising speed under reduced gravity / reduced effective body weight, and accepted thresholds

### Takeaway
The Froude number is Fr = v² / (g·L), with v walking speed, g gravitational acceleration and L leg length (usually greater-trochanter height or leg length). It is the standard dimensionless speed for comparing gait across body sizes and gravity levels. The accepted thresholds are **Fr ≈ 0.25 for comfortable/preferred walking** and **Fr ≈ 0.5 for the walk–run transition**; the 0.5 transition threshold holds down to about 0.4 g and then rises steeply, reaching ~0.9 at lunar gravity. Dynamic similarity at equal Fr holds for duty factor but **fails** for relative stride length, which is a material caveat.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: "The Froude number here is v²/(gL), with v the speed, g gravity, and L leg length" — [Transition from walking to running, Wikipedia summary of the literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_from_walking_to_running) (tertiary source for the formula, which is uncontroversial)
- **Walk–run transition threshold:** "Humans make the transition from walking to running at a Froude number around 0.5, even under conditions simulating reduced gravity" — [same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_from_walking_to_running)
- Primary source for the threshold's gravity-invariance: Kram and colleagues (1997), *J Exp Biol* 200(4):821–826 — under simulated reduced gravity, subjects switched to running at progressively slower *absolute* speeds but at approximately the *same* Froude number; the authors read this as support for an inverted-pendulum trigger for the transition — ["Effect of reduced gravity on the preferred walk-run transition speed," J Exp Biol](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article/200/4/821/7425/Effect-of-reduced-gravity-on-the-preferred-walk)
- **Where the 0.5 threshold breaks down:** Carr & McGee (2009), *PLoS ONE* — "Human unsuited Fr* is relatively constant (∼0.5) with gravity but increases substantially with decreasing gravity below ∼0.4 g, rising to 0.9 in 1/6 g; space suits appear to lower Fr*." Their Apollo-footage estimate for *suited* walkers gave Fr* = 0.36 ± 0.11 (mean ± 95% CI) from 38 gait events, and an "Apollo number" Ap* = 0.68 ± 0.20 — [The Apollo Number: Space Suits, Self-Support, and the Walk-Run Transition (PMC2719915)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2719915/); also [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/52418)
- **Comfortable-walking Froude number used as a protocol parameter:** a 12-subject study comparing supine lower body *negative* pressure and upright LBPP for lunar/Martian gravity simulation used "a protocol of resting and walking (0.25 Froude)"; during walking, all cardiovascular and biomechanical parameters were unaffected by which simulation modality was used for lunar and Martian gravities — [Refubium, FU Berlin](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/handle/fub188/3029)
- **The dynamic-similarity caveat:** Donelan & Kram (1997), *J Exp Biol* — using a device that pushes up on the torso during treadmill walking to test dynamic similarity across simulated gravity levels, at equal Froude numbers subjects walked with nearly the same **duty factor**, but **relative stride lengths differed by as much as 67%**, leading the authors to reject their dynamic-similarity hypothesis — ["The effect of reduced gravity on the kinematics of human walking: a test of the dynamic similarity hypothesis for locomotion" (SFU Locomotion Lab PDF)](https://www.sfu.ca/locomotionlab/assets/dynsimwalk1997.pdf)
- Related reduced-gravity mechanics: Griffin TM, Tolani NA, Kram R. "Walking in simulated reduced gravity: mechanical energy fluctuations and exchange." *J Appl Physiol* 1999 — [Colorado Experts record](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_105203) (record lists authors and journal; no findings retrievable)
- Example of Froude-equivalent protocol design in LBPP running: a study compared Earth-like vs simulated Mars-like gravity (100% vs 38% BW) during running on an LBPP treadmill and found that BW reduction induced biomechanical adaptations independently of the visual context — [Front Physiol 2023;14:1201253](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1201253/pdf)

### Inferences
- **Critical distinction for this application:** the Froude number contains *g*, not effective body weight. On an LBPP or harness treadmill, gravity is unchanged — what changes is the net vertical support force. The two are not interchangeable: reduced g scales both the gravitational force *and* the inertia-to-weight ratio of every body segment, whereas BWS reduces the net upward force at the pelvis/torso while leaving every segment's inertia and the g acting on the swinging limbs fully intact. Reduced-gravity Froude thresholds therefore transfer to BWS treadmills only approximately, and the literature's habit of labelling BWS conditions "simulated reduced gravity" (as several of the sources above do, e.g. "reduced-gravity conditions of 40% and 20% body weight") conflates them. This is worth stating explicitly in the report.
- The practical convention in the LBPP/reduced-gravity literature is to use an *effective gravity* g_eff = (offload fraction) × g in the Froude expression, which yields Fr = v²/(g_eff·L). At a fixed belt speed, reducing effective weight therefore *raises* the effective Froude number, which is the mechanism behind the observed drop in stance fraction and earlier walk-run transition at high offload. But because segment inertia is unchanged, this substitution is an approximation, and Donelan & Kram's 67% stride-length discrepancy at matched Fr shows the approximation already fails for real reduced gravity, let alone for BWS.
- For a console: normalising speed as Fr = v²/(g·L) with *true* g and measured leg length gives a defensible size-independent speed, with Fr ≈ 0.25 marking comfortable walking and Fr ≈ 0.5 the walk-run transition. Using g_eff instead should be labelled as such, because it will produce Froude numbers above 0.5 at ordinary walking speeds under deep offload, and the resulting "should be running" prediction will often be wrong.

### Gaps
- No source found that directly validates Froude-number normalisation for **BWS/LBPP** specifically (as opposed to true or simulated reduced gravity), i.e. no study that tested whether gait at matched Fr under different offloads is dynamically similar.
- The walk–run transition Froude number *on an LBPP treadmill at defined offload levels* was not found; the 0.5 threshold evidence comes from harness-type reduced-gravity simulators (Kram 1997) and Apollo video analysis (Carr & McGee 2009).
- The Fr ≈ 0.25 comfortable-walking figure is cited here from a protocol description rather than from a primary study establishing it; a dedicated source for the preferred-walking Froude number would strengthen it.
- Griffin, Tolani & Kram (1999) could not be retrieved in full; its mechanical-energy-exchange results would be relevant to whether the inverted-pendulum mechanism is preserved under unloading.

## Cross-cutting notes for the report writer

- **Sample sizes across the entire retrieved literature:** n = 4 (Patil instrumented knee), 8, 10, 10, 14, 15, 15+15, 18, 20, 25, 26, 27, 29, 30 (the one RCT), 31. Nothing larger was found. Every quantitative relationship in these notes rests on a cohort of ≤ 31 people, usually healthy young adults, usually at a single lab, on one device.
- **Manufacturer provenance, flagged items:** the AlterG Clinical Research Summary (D001121 Rev A, © 2025 Lifeward Inc.) is the sole source here for the Patil 2013 claim and for the Peeler 2015/2018, Kawae 2017 and Webber 2014 summaries. The Patil study itself was AlterG-sponsored (confirmed by the Scripps press release). AlterG's universal claim that "gait mechanics are maintained for all levels of weight support and speed" is contradicted by Phillips 2025 (significant stance, cadence, stride changes at 75% BW in jogging/running) and by the hip OA study (p < 0.001 BW effects on hip, knee and ankle peak angles at 75% and 50% BW).
- **Conflicts and inconsistencies to report rather than silently resolve:** (1) loading rate unchanged under harness BWS (Barela 2014, p > 0.5) vs loading rate decreasing linearly under LBPP (BMC 2021) — possibly a real mechanism difference, possibly a measurement difference; (2) the sign of the McNeill 2015 deep-offload error is reported inconsistently across secondary summaries; (3) the Sensors 2021 paper's Conclusions contradict its own Results on COP length and width; (4) the De Heer 2021 abstract's 24.1% spread does not match its own 71.2/48.1 figures; (5) the Phillips 2025 impact-force units are implausible as newtons.
- **What metrics are defensible to report when a patient is partially unloaded** (synthesis, grounded in the findings above): measured delivered load as % of true body weight (per stride, not from the dial); peak vGRF as % BW *and* in absolute units; weight-acceptance/loading rate (because it does **not** scale with offload under harness support and so carries independent information); push-off rate (which does scale); stance fraction and double-support fraction *at a stated belt speed*, with the caveat that offload moves them by <1 percentage point per 25% offload during walking; and the minimum offload at which the patient walks pain-free, session over session (the Peeler knee OA dose-reduction observation). Metrics to avoid or caveat heavily: anything that requires detecting two vGRF peaks or a mid-stance trough (unreliable above ~15–30% offload as the waveform flattens); joint-force estimates inferred from GRF alone; and any parameter compared against overground norms without a treadmill correction.
