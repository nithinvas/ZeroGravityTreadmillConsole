# Clinical Use of Gait Metrics and Presentation of Gait Data to Physiotherapists (Post-Surgical and Arthritis Rehabilitation)

**Scope note on source quality:** Most findings below were obtained via web search with full-text verification on a subset. Where a figure comes only from a search snippet or a secondary summary (toolkit, conference abstract, vendor blog), this is flagged inline. Two guideline PDFs could not be retrieved (403 / members-only) and are recorded as gaps.

## Q1. Which gait metrics are established clinical outcome measures in physiotherapy for lower-limb post-surgical rehabilitation and for knee/hip OA?

### Takeaway
The striking finding is that **guideline-recommended outcome measures in this population are almost never gait metrics**. The APTA's total knee arthroplasty CPG recommends a patient-reported measure plus two non-gait performance tests (30-second sit-to-stand and Timed Up and Go); gait speed appears in the TKA guideline only as a research outcome, not as a recommended clinical measure. Where walking *is* a recommended measure, it is a **timed walk test** (gait speed derived from a fixed distance or fixed time), not instrumented spatiotemporal or kinetic variables.

### Cited Findings
- The APTA's 2020 TKA clinical practice guideline (Jette et al., *Physical Therapy* 100(9):1603–1631) reached consensus that physical therapists should use the **KOOS JR** as the patient-reported measure and the **30-Second Sit-to-Stand** and **Timed "Up and Go" (TUG)** as the performance-based measures, collected at minimum at the first visit and at discharge from each care setting. The recommendation is graded **"Best Practice"** with evidence quality rated **"Insufficient"** — [APTA TKA CPG full text, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7462050)
- The same guideline contains **no dedicated gait-assessment recommendation**. Gait speed and stair-climbing time appear only as outcomes of included studies. The 6-Minute Walk Test, Berg Balance Scale, Five Times Sit-to-Stand, Stair-Climbing Test and balance tests likewise appear only as study outcomes, not recommended measures — [APTA TKA CPG full text, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7462050)
- The guideline covers primary TKA for knee OA and **excludes** revisions, partial arthroplasties, TKA after trauma, and TKA for rheumatoid arthritis. Recommendations are graded Strong / Moderate / Weak / Best Practice Statement — [APTA news release](https://apta.org/news/2020/06/22/first-ever-apta-clinical-practice-guideline-targets-tka)
- The JOSPT hip OA CPG (2025 revision, "Hip Pain and Mobility Deficits — Hip Osteoarthritis") recommends clinicians use **validated outcome measures spanning hip pain, body-function impairment, activity limitation and participation restriction**, and lists "essential data elements" including both self-reported and physical performance measures (carried forward from the 2017 recommendation). *Snippet-level only — the PDF returned HTTP 403 on fetch* — [JOSPT/AOPT hip OA CPG 2025 PDF](https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/Hip_pain_and_mobility_deficits_hip_osteoarthritis_2025.pdf)
- **OARSI recommends the 40-metre Fast-Paced Walk Test (40mFPWT)** to assess physical function in adults with knee OA; the 6MWT is also an OARSI-recommended measure with reported good validity, reliability and responsiveness — [OARSI performance test adoption summary, HCPLive](https://www.hcplive.com/view/oarsi-adopts-tests-physical-function-hip-and-knee-oa); [40mFPWT toolkit summary, UBC](https://med-fom-clone-pt.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2024/01/40-metre-Fast-paced-Walk-Test-40mFPWT-.pdf); [6MWT toolkit summary, UBC](https://med-fom-clone-pt.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2024/01/Six-Minute-Walk-Test-6MWT-.pdf)
- A narrative review in the *Journal of Rheumatology* argues walking speed **should** be assessed in clinical practice for knee OA because it has prognostic value and is modifiable, and notes slow walking speed is linked to increased risk of adverse health outcomes including all-cause mortality in adults with knee OA. The same review found **little consensus on the distance and instructions** used for the walk test, but good reliability and validity across protocols for fixed-distance walk tests of ≤40 m, whether fast- or self-paced — [J Rheumatol 48(5):638](https://www.jrheum.org/content/48/5/638)
- For ACL reconstruction, the dominant clinical measures are **not gait metrics at all** but strength and hop-test limb symmetry: a scoping review found LSI ≥90% versus the contralateral limb is the most commonly reported functional return-to-sport metric, most commonly assessed via isokinetic quadriceps strength, then single-leg vertical hop and cross-over hop — [Frontiers in Medicine 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2024.1303172/full)
- Gait kinetics after ACLR are a **research**, not routine-clinical, variable. Quadriceps strength symmetry was **not** correlated with persistent asymmetries in gait biomechanics, leading authors to conclude restoring strength alone may not resolve gait asymmetry — [PMC6170000](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6170000)

### Inferences
- A rehabilitation treadmill console that displays gait speed, cadence, step length and symmetry is displaying variables that are **mostly not in any CPG's recommended measure set**. The commercially and clinically defensible framing is therefore "gait speed as the walk-test substitute" (where a guideline/OARSI anchor exists) plus symmetry/loading as *process* feedback for treatment, not as an outcome measure.
- Of all the metrics a load-cell treadmill can produce, **gait speed has by far the strongest claim to being a recognised clinical measure**, because it is the derived quantity of the recommended timed walk tests (40mFPWT, 6MWT, 10mWT) and has independent prognostic literature.
- Because the recommended measures (30s sit-to-stand, TUG) are **not treadmill-measurable**, a console cannot replace the clinician's outcome battery; it can at best complement it. Designing the console as if it were the outcome-measurement system would misrepresent current practice.
- To be interoperable with clinician practice, a treadmill-derived speed should be reportable in a **walk-test-comparable protocol** (fixed distance or fixed duration, self-paced vs fast-paced explicitly labelled) rather than only as an arbitrary steady-state treadmill speed.

### Gaps
- The JOSPT/AOPT **knee** OA clinical practice guideline and the JOSPT **knee ligament sprain / ACL** CPG ("Knee Stability and Movement Coordination Impairments") were not retrieved; their specific recommended-measure lists and any gait-related recommendations are unverified.
- No CPG was found for **cartilage repair** rehabilitation. The post-surgical cartilage-repair literature was not located within this search budget; treat cartilage repair as unresolved.
- The 2025 hip OA CPG's "essential data elements" list could not be read directly (403), so it is unknown whether it names gait speed explicitly.
- APTA's CPG+ on 3D instrumented gait analysis is members-only; its reporting recommendations were not reviewable.

## Q2. What recognised thresholds do clinicians use? (ACL LSI for return to sport; clinically significant gait asymmetry)

### Takeaway
**LSI ≥90% is the de facto threshold** for return to sport after ACLR, but it is explicitly not consensus-backed, it over-estimates recovery because the uninvolved limb also weakens, and very few patients (or even healthy athletes) achieve it across a full battery. For *gait* asymmetry, there is no equivalent well-established post-surgical threshold; the closest validated figure is a **10% asymmetry cutoff for "pathological" asymmetry, validated in hip OA**, not in arthroplasty or ACLR.

### Cited Findings
**ACL / limb symmetry index**
- LSI ≥90% is the most commonly reported functional return-to-sport criterion after ACLR, but no consensus exists on the test battery; current RTS criteria are described as "still arbitrary and often time-based" — [Frontiers in Medicine 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2024.1303172/full)
- Symmetry at the 90% level is uncommon even in uninjured people: only **17% of ACLR athletes and 24% of non-injured athletes** met the symmetry criterion on all four hop/strength tests. Knee flexion strength was the only measure where the ACLR group was clearly less symmetric. The authors argue a 90% cutoff alone may be **too strict to serve as a gate** — [PMC9808831](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9808831/)
- LSI timing: at 6 months post-ACLR, LSI ≥90% was reached by 21.2% (counter-movement jump), 36.4% (drop jump) and 33.3% (side-hop); by 1 year these rose to 66.6%, 63.6% and 81.8% respectively (n=33) — [Frontiers in Medicine 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2024.1303172/full)
- Wellsandt et al. (2017, *JOSPT*): "limb symmetry indexes **frequently overestimate** knee function after ACLR and may be related to second ACL injury risk," because **bilateral** strength deficits occur after ACL injury, undermining the uninvolved limb as a reference — [PubMed 28355978](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28355978/)
- A 2026 *Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine* cross-sectional study used a clinic-feasible battery requiring ≥90% LSI on isometric quadriceps strength, 1RM isotonic knee extension and four single-leg hops (single, crossover, triple, 6-m timed), plus ≥90% on Global Rating Scale and IKDC-2000. Athletes who **passed** still averaged **7.6% lower** isometric quadriceps strength on the injured limb, nearly **30% still had <80% LSI** on isometric testing, and **almost half failed the 3 Nm/kg** isometric knee-extension torque threshold — [OJSM 14(2), 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/23259671251410098)
- Absolute (not symmetry) thresholds are recommended as a complement: ACLR-limb isometric quadriceps strength of **3.0 Nm/kg** plus LSI ≥90% have been associated with acceptable patient-reported function — [PMC10496454](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10496454)
- Quadriceps strength is the most frequently failed criterion: one study found it unmet in **71%** of participants; another found **85%** of individuals 5–7 months post-ACLR failed either the quadriceps strength or the symmetry criterion — [PMC10496454](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10496454); [NATA/J Athl Train 58(6):536](https://nata.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/attr/58/6/article-p536.xml)

**Gait asymmetry thresholds**
- A general **10% cutoff for pathological asymmetry** was tested in hip OA (trunk and footfall symmetry) and found to have "high total classification ability," to not misclassify able-bodied subjects as pathological, and to be "feasible for use on individual patients in the clinic as well as in research." This is a **diagnostic cutoff for a single assessment, not a threshold for meaningful change** — [PubMed 21722001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21722001)
- Healthy-gait symmetry benchmarks: in 737 children and 82 young adults on a GAITRite walkway, mean left–right differences were ≈**0.8 cm** for step and stride length and ≈**0.7%** for stance, step, swing and support timing; the authors concluded gait is "highly symmetrical" in healthy young people and that this "appears to be an invariant quality of human gait but may change with pathology" — [Lythgo et al., IFMBE Proc. 2010](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-14515-5_46)
- There is **no accepted standard symmetry formula**. Patterson et al. (2010, *Gait & Posture*) compared symmetry index, symmetry ratio, gait asymmetry and symmetry angle for step length, swing time and stance time in 161 stroke survivors and 81 healthy adults, concluded that "there is no accepted standard for assessing symmetry making it difficult to compare across studies and establish criteria to guide clinical decision making," and **recommended the symmetry ratio** for standardisation — [McMaster Experts record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1644239)
- A competing recommendation: Błażkiewicz et al. (2014) analysed 58 healthy young adults and argued the **symmetry index** is the most sensitive spatiotemporal symmetry measure, publishing normative per-feature values. *Direct conflict with Patterson et al. on which formula to standardise on; the actual SI numbers were not retrievable from the search result* — [Acta Bioeng Biomech 16(1), 2014](https://actabio.pwr.edu.pl/fcp/DGBUKOQtTKlQhbx08SlkTUARAUWRuHQwFDBoIVURNWHhUFVZpCFghUHcKVj0GQUw/302/public/publikacje/v16-1-2014/4.pdf)
- Post-TKA loading asymmetry does not persist uniformly: one small study found preoperative loading asymmetry during walking but **no significant postoperative loading asymmetries**, while asymmetry during **quiet stance persisted to 6 months** — [PMC5450932](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5450932/)

### Inferences
- A console that reports a symmetry number **must state which formula it uses**, because the literature explicitly cannot be compared across formulas. Reporting "symmetry: 92%" without the equation is uninterpretable against any published threshold.
- The validated clinical thresholds in this space (90% LSI, 10% asymmetry) are **single-timepoint classification gates**, not change thresholds. A console that highlights week-to-week changes against a 10% line would be misusing a diagnostic cutoff.
- Because Lythgo's healthy left–right differences are ~0.7–0.8% / 0.8 cm, a 10% asymmetry flag is far above healthy noise — meaning a console's symmetry display has reasonable headroom, but also that small displayed asymmetries (1–5%) are within normal human variation and should not be rendered as findings.
- The strongest evidence-based argument *against* over-weighting symmetry on a console is Wellsandt's bilateral-deficit problem: symmetry can be perfect while both limbs are weak. A console displaying only symmetry (and not an absolute load or absolute speed) can show "normal" for a patient who is bilaterally deconditioned.

### Gaps
- No published MCID or MDC was found for a *gait* limb symmetry index in ACLR, TKA or THA from a peer-reviewed full-text source (see Q3 for low-confidence conference-abstract figures).
- No formal consensus statement from a sports medicine society establishing the 90% LSI gate was located; the 90% figure appears to be convention rather than guideline.

## Q3. MCID and MDC values for gait speed, cadence, step length and symmetry in these populations

### Takeaway
**Gait speed has usable MCID/MDC values** (0.05 m/s small / 0.10 m/s substantial in older adults; ~0.07–0.115 m/s in knee OA; PASS of 1.2 m/s after TKA), though the TKA literature explicitly failed to establish an MCID for walking speed. **Cadence, step length and symmetry have essentially no established MCID/MDC in these populations** — the only candidate values found are 2026 AAOS conference abstracts whose reported units appear implausible and which should not be used.

### Cited Findings
**Gait speed — general / older adults**
- Perera et al. (2006) in older adults: "best initial estimates of **small meaningful change are near 0.05 m/s** for gait speed … and of **substantial change are near 0.10 m/s**" — [PubMed 16696738](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16696738/)
- These thresholds do not transfer universally: a hip-fracture study in women found only limited support for the 0.10 m/s substantial-change cut point and suggested meaningful change "may differ based on the direction of change (improvement vs decline) or between patient populations" — [PMC3230716](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3230716)
- Population-specific values differ: one compilation lists 0.05 m/s small / 0.10 m/s substantial for post-stroke and geriatric populations, 0.13 m/s for spinal cord injury, 0.15 m/s for traumatic brain injury — [Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, Gait Speed](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/gait-speed)

**Gait speed — knee OA**
- Gilbert et al. (2021, Osteoarthritis Initiative data, radiographic knee OA): combined distribution-based and prevalent anchor-based important differences were **4.1–6.9 m/min for the 20 m walk** and **2.9–6.9 m/min for the 400 m walk** (≈0.068–0.115 m/s and ≈0.048–0.115 m/s respectively — *unit conversion is mine, not the source's*). The authors deemed their **longitudinal anchor-based estimates unreliable** — [PMC7392790](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7392790); [ACR abstract](https://acrabstracts.org/abstract/what-is-an-important-difference-in-gait-speed-in-adults-with-knee-osteoarthritis/)
- 40mFPWT in knee/hip OA: **MDC 0.19 m/s**; smallest detectable change **0.22 m/s** (hip OA); minimal clinically important improvement **0.2–0.3 m/s** (hip OA). For **TKA/THA the same toolkit states "No evidence found"** for cut points/thresholds. *Secondary toolkit summary; primary studies not verified* — [40mFPWT toolkit, UBC](https://med-fom-clone-pt.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2024/01/40-metre-Fast-paced-Walk-Test-40mFPWT-.pdf)

**Gait speed — after TKA**
- A 2020 study concluded for walking speed and maximal knee flexion during gait after TKA that **"Minimal Clinically Important Improvement Is Not Determinable; Patient Acceptable Symptom State Is Potentially Useful"** — changes in gait outcomes were not associated with perceived body function or patient satisfaction, precluding MCII estimation. It did establish a **patient acceptable symptom state (PASS) of 1.2 m/s** for walking speed — [CIRRIS record](https://www.cirris.ulaval.ca/publications/walking-speed-and-maximal-knee-flexion-during-gait-after-total-knee-arthroplasty-minimal-clinically-important-improvement-is-not-determinable-patient-acceptable-symptom-state-is-potentially-useful); [Univ. Geneva archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:141908)

**Other timed-test MDCs in TKA (for context on what clinicians currently track)**
- TKA (n=48, 2017, *J Arthroplasty*): **TUG SEM 0.82 s, MDC95 2.27 s**; **2-Minute Walk Test SEM 5.40 m, MDC95 14.96 m** — [MCBU record](https://avesis.mcbu.edu.tr/yayin/8def8918-af68-4fe1-9075-acdc8c4c769d/assessing-minimal-detectable-changes-and-test-retest-reliability-of-the-timed-up-and-go-test-and-the-2-minute-walk-test-in-patients-with-total-knee-arthroplasty)
- 6MWT: reported **MCID 74.36 m at 12 months post-TKA**; **MDC90 61.34 m** for TKA/THA; from pre-op to 6 months post-TKA, "slight" and "more" improvement corresponded to **26 m and 55 m** respectively. *Secondary toolkit summary* — [6MWT toolkit, UBC](https://med-fom-clone-pt.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2024/01/Six-Minute-Walk-Test-6MWT-.pdf)
- Four-performance-measure longitudinal study after total hip and knee arthroplasty (Kennedy et al.) is the primary source the toolkits cite — [PMC549207](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC549207/)

**Cadence, step length, symmetry — low-confidence conference-abstract figures (DO NOT USE)**
- AAOS 2026 abstracts (Redfern et al.) on wearable-derived gait after knee arthroplasty report, for TKA, distribution-based **MDC95 values of 0.082 m/s (gait speed), 1,461 steps, 0.052 m (step length), 0.028% and 0.156% (walking asymmetry)**, with an MCID for gait speed of **0.067 m/s**. For unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, MCID for walking asymmetry **0.7%** and MDC95 **0.127%** — [AAOS 2026 abstract 008974](https://index.mirasmart.com/AAOS2026/PDFfiles/AAOS2026-008974.pdf); [AAOS 2026 abstract 008979](https://index.mirasmart.com/AAOS2026/PDFfiles/AAOS2026-008979.pdf)
  - **Reliability warning:** these are unreviewed conference abstracts; the asymmetry MDC95 of 0.156% is *smaller* than the ~0.7% left–right difference observed in healthy young adults (Lythgo et al., above), which is implausible for a detectable-change statistic. The mapping of values to parameters was inferred from listing order in the abstract. Treat all of these as unverified.
- No MDC for **step length, cadence or step-time symmetry in knee OA** was found. The nearest knee-OA reliability work concerns the Step Test (a balance test: ICC 0.97, SEM 0.46, MDC95 1.27) and the five-time sit-to-stand and 5 m walk tests — [Physiother Quarterly 2022](https://physioquart.awf.wroc.pl/Reliability-and-minimal-detectable-change-of-the-Step-Test-in-patients-with-knee,125074,0,2.html); [Prog Rehabil Med 2018](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/prm/3/0/3_20180022/_article/-char/en)
- Descriptive (not MDC) knee-OA gait profile: "significant decrease in cadence, step length, stride length and walking speed, while step time, stride time, stance time, double support time were longer in the patients" — [PMC3661341](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3661341)
- Cadence has weak prognostic value in knee OA: using GAITRite data from the Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study, **lower cadence was not significantly associated** with incident or worsening knee symptoms, pain with walking, or functional limitations — [Univ. of Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Association-of-Walking-Cadence-to-Changes/9984786445502771)

**Normative gait speed context**
- Bohannon & Andrews meta-analysis of comfortable walking speed (41 articles, 23,111 subjects) is the standard normative reference; derived tables give ≈**1.43 m/s** for men in their 30s–50s, ≈1.39 m/s for women in their 40s, falling to ≈**1.26 m/s (men) / 1.13 m/s (women) in the 70s** and ≈**0.97 / 0.94 m/s at 80+** — [Univ. of Toronto iWalk normative values PDF](https://www.physicaltherapy.utoronto.ca/sites/default/files/assets/files/11walking-speed-age-and-sex-specific-normative-values-final.pdf); [Bohannon & Andrews, descriptive meta-analysis](https://bacpar.csp.org.uk/node/338279)
- A larger meta-analysis of 51,248 healthy adults found mean speeds from **97 cm/s (women 80+) to 140 cm/s (men 40–49)**, with women declining steadily and men flat until their 40s — [Australian Physiotherapy Association summary](https://australian.physio/node/3149)
- Caveat on applying norms: the Bohannon data may not apply when gait is timed over short distances starting from a "go" command, or when the walk includes a turn — [Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, Gait Speed](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/gait-speed)

### Inferences
- Only **gait speed** can be responsibly shown on a console with a change threshold attached. A sensible, defensible design is to annotate speed change against 0.05 m/s (small) and 0.10 m/s (substantial), explicitly labelled as derived from community-dwelling older adults, and optionally 1.2 m/s as a post-TKA PASS target.
- For cadence, step length and symmetry the honest design position is: **show the value and the trend, do not show a "clinically meaningful" badge**, because no MDC/MCID exists to support one. Anything a console does display as "significant change" for these variables would be invented.
- The TKA finding that gait changes were **not associated with perceived function or satisfaction** is a direct warning against a console that treats gait improvement as the headline measure of rehabilitation success after TKA.
- The large gap between the 40mFPWT MDC (0.19 m/s) and the 0.05/0.10 m/s older-adult thresholds suggests that a single treadmill-derived speed reading carries real measurement noise; a console should prefer averaged/repeated measures over single-session point values.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed MDC or MCID for gait symmetry, cadence or step length in ACLR, TKA, THA or cartilage repair was found. This is a genuine hole in the literature, not a search failure — the only candidates are the unreviewed AAOS 2026 abstracts.
- MCID/MDC values specific to **total hip arthroplasty** gait speed were not isolated; the toolkit values pool TKA/THA.
- No MCID/MDC values were found for any cartilage-repair population.

## Q4. How is weight-bearing progression prescribed and monitored, what %BW do the categories mean, and how accurately do patients comply without feedback?

### Takeaway
The weight-bearing categories are **terminologically ambiguous** (clinicians disagree on what each term means in %BW), and compliance without feedback is **very poor** — in a 50-patient TKA study using instrumented insoles, 0% of patients complied on day 1 and 2% at discharge, with 84–92% overloading on *every step*. Patients typically loaded ~50–57% of body weight against a 20 kg prescription. This is the single strongest evidence base relevant to a load-cell console.

### Cited Findings
**What the categories mean**
- Toe-touch weight bearing (TTWB) is defined as the foot or toes touching the floor for balance without supporting weight — [Wikipedia, Weight-bearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight-bearing)
- A *BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine* paper states **TTWB is generally considered as 20% total body weight (TBW) or 10–15 kg**, and **"most authors use a definition of PWB as 30%–50% TBW"** — [BMJ Open SEM 4(1):e000326](https://bmjopensem.bmj.com/content/4/1/e000326.abstract)
- The same authors recommend that "the terms should not be used in isolation without a further descriptor (ie, **PWB at 40%**)" for consistency in rehabilitation delivery — [BMJ Open SEM 4(1):e000326](https://bmjopensem.bmj.com/content/4/1/e000326.abstract)
- A UK survey of **707 trauma and orthopaedic professionals** found **89% interpret "touch/toe-touch WB" as 10% or 20% BW** and **80% interpret partial weight-bearing as 50%**, with **doctors tending to give higher %BW values than therapists** — [QMUL repository record](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/handle/123456789/92939); [PubMed 29629184](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29629184/)
- A device patent (convention, not clinical standard) defines TTWB as **<20 lbf** and PWB as **30% BW** unless the physician specifies otherwise — [US6273863](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6273863)

**Compliance without feedback**
- **Prospective TKA study, 50 patients, instrumented insoles (Loadsol, calibrated to each patient's body weight):** prescribed limit **200 N (~20 kg)** with crutches. Compliance (defined as overloading in <10% of steps) was **0% on post-op day 1 and 2% the day before discharge** (one patient, hallway and stairs only). **84%** of patients overloaded on *every step* on day 1, **90%** in the hallway at discharge, **92%** on stairs. Mean body weight loaded on the operated limb: **50% ± 14%** (day 1 hallway), **57% ± 17%** (discharge hallway), **66% ± 21%** (stairs up), **59% ± 20%** (stairs down). Mean maximum load per step: **401 ± 140 N** (day 1 operated limb) vs **765 ± 174 N** contralateral. Patients **over 65 loaded the limb 17% more on day 1 and 34% more at discharge** (7.9 N/kg vs 5.3 N/kg), and **49%/53% more going up/down stairs**. The authors call the 200 N protocol **"senseless" and "not feasible for the majority of patients after TKA,"** noting crutches reduce load only to about half body weight in younger patients and that 200 N is realistic only for patients weighing ≤40 kg — [PMC10439061](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10439061/) *(full text verified)*
- **Surgeon-simulated protocols:** success at following weight-bearing orders was **40% for TTWB measured in kilograms vs 58% as %BW**, and **22% for PWB in kilograms vs 50% as %BW**. The authors conclude **percentage-based orders are followed more accurately** and call for standardised weight-bearing orders — [Orthobullets evidence record (abstract)](https://orthobullets.com/evidence/20954673)
- **Healthy volunteers taught 20/50/80% protocols on bathroom scales:** static accuracy was reasonable, but during crutch walking on a force plate the **peak error at 20% BW was double the intended load**; error shrank as the target rose, and at **80% BW the peak error was not significantly different from zero** — [Adelaide repository record](https://web4.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/93259?mode=full)
- **Elderly proximal femoral fracture patients:** most patients (**≥72%**) exceeded their target load, with **mean peak loading reaching 285% of target**; patients and physiotherapists could not accurately judge partial weight-bearing accuracy — [Bradford Scholars record](https://bradscholars.brad.ac.uk/entities/publication/3883d0e1-40e3-4eb2-9632-a2df907eea1e)
- A further study reported patients **could not follow weight-bearing restrictions after instruction and practice on scales**, and could not reproduce target levels in static or dynamic conditions — [Orthobullets evidence record](https://orthobullets.com/evidence/20954673)
- Bathroom scales, the standard-of-care teaching tool, are explicitly criticised for inaccuracy in the PWB-training literature — [IJSPT 2025](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/129259-the-impact-of-real-time-biofeedback-on-partial-weightbearing-training-a-comparative-study)

**Accuracy of existing measurement devices**
- Validity against a force plate differed by device: agreement was **substantial for SmartStep, moderate for OpenGo Science, slight for SensiStep**, and **none of the devices showed acceptable agreement in the >50%–75% range** — [UMC Utrecht research record](https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/biofeedback-in-partial-weight-bearing-validity-of-3-different-dev/)

### Inferences
- The strongest clinical case for a load-cell treadmill console is **not** spatiotemporal gait metrics but **weight-bearing load display**, because the compliance evidence shows a near-total failure of unaided PWB and the cause (patients cannot perceive load) is directly addressable by measurement.
- A console should express load as **%BW and in Newtons/kg simultaneously**, because clinicians disagree on category definitions and the evidence says %BW orders are followed more accurately than kg orders. Displaying only a category label ("PWB") would reproduce the ambiguity the BMJ authors warn against.
- Because patients over 65 load substantially more, and because crutch-assisted gait floors at ~50% BW in younger patients, a console should treat very low prescriptions (e.g. 20 kg) as **likely unachievable** and should probably surface that rather than show a permanently-failing target.
- A console should show **peak load per step and the proportion of steps in overload**, not just a mean, because the TKA study's clinically meaningful outcome was "% of steps overloaded" and the mean masks per-step violations.
- Device-validity findings imply a console should **state its own agreement with force-plate reference**, especially in the >50% BW range where existing insole devices fail.

### Gaps
- No evidence was found on whether exceeding a prescribed weight-bearing limit actually causes worse clinical outcomes (fixation failure, non-union) — i.e. whether the thing a console would enforce matters. The TKA authors imply the prescription itself may be unjustified.
- The UK survey's full distribution of %BW interpretations (beyond the 89%/80% headline figures) was not retrieved.

## Q5. Does real-time biofeedback of ground reaction force or symmetry improve outcomes or weight-bearing compliance?

### Takeaway
**Yes for weight-bearing compliance**, with randomised evidence — but mostly in **healthy volunteers**, not patients. For **clinical outcomes** after TKA, a 2019 systematic review of 11 studies (416 patients) found 9 of 11 reported significant improvement in activity scores or pain, but the trials are small and heterogeneous. For **gait/GRF retraining after ACLR**, evidence is preliminary (n≈19, confidence intervals crossing zero).

### Cited Findings
**Weight-bearing compliance (randomised)**
- **Sensors 2024 RCT, healthy adults**, insole orthosis, 15–30 kg target: without biofeedback, "on level ground and stairs, only one in two steps fell within the target area, and overloading occurred on at least one in three steps." The biofeedback group **reduced steps in the overload zone to ≤8.4%**, with significant target-zone gains on level ground and stairs; the **control group showed no significant change**. The authors conclude ambulatory biofeedback systems outperform bathroom scales, and note that **no high-quality studies had previously examined ambulatory biofeedback devices** — [Sensors 24(19):6443](https://mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/19/6443/htm); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/373c26add3244e779a1235f13b2f3d0f)
- **IJSPT 2025, 60 healthy participants**, 20 kg PWB target: biofeedback group mean peak force **330 N** vs **600 N** in the standard-of-care group, which exceeded the 20 kg limit by more than threefold (**p ≤ 0.001**) — [IJSPT article 129259](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/129259-the-impact-of-real-time-biofeedback-on-partial-weightbearing-training-a-comparative-study); [Augsburg repository PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/121008/121008.pdf)
- **RCT in healthy adults comparing an ambulatory biofeedback device vs bathroom-scale instruction** — [PMC11479348](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11479348/)
- **Springer 2024 RCT in healthy volunteers aged ≥60**, audio-biofeedback vs bathroom scale, adherence defined as mean load 15–25 kg; *results not retrieved* — [Eur J Trauma Emerg Surg, 10.1007/s00068-024-02609-5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00068-024-02609-5)
- **Applied Sciences 2022, 30 patients after TKA:** authors reported that "partial weight bearing with a correct gait in the post-operative period is **not obtainable without a measuring system**." *Small, non-randomised — weak evidence* — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/f9c27274ab064bdeae6183f4944dc1b1)
- An earlier study of 33 patients in 2 groups found subjects with feedback **were able to follow weight-bearing instructions better**; design unclear from the available record — [COMFORT protocol background, PMC5898035](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5898035/)
- A review noted the prior evidence level was **low**, "primarily due to a limited number of cases, the lack of randomization, and a heterogeneous sample of participants" — predating the 2024–25 RCTs — [Applied Sciences 2022 / DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/f9c27274ab064bdeae6183f4944dc1b1)

**Clinical outcomes after TKA**
- **Pfeufer et al. 2019, *KSSTA* 27(5):1611–1620**, "Training with biofeedback devices improves clinical outcome compared to usual care in patients with unilateral TKA": 380 studies screened, **11 included (7 RCTs, 3 cohort, 1 cross-sectional), 416 unilateral TKA patients**. **9 of 11 studies reported significant improvements in activity scores or pain**; only 2 reported no significant influence — [LMU repository record](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81236/)
- **Karaborklu Argut et al. 2022**, EMG biofeedback after orthopaedic knee surgery: 8 RCTs; most reported EMG biofeedback more effective than home exercise, standard rehabilitation or electrical stimulation for **quadriceps strength or activation** — [Inonu Univ. record](https://avesis.inonu.edu.tr/yayin/62a184ae-f70f-4eea-a6e8-13bf6bf18ca4/effectiveness-of-therapeutic-electromyographic-biofeedback-after-orthopedic-knee-surgeries-a-systematic-review)
- **Christiansen et al. (weight-bearing biofeedback after TKA), n=26**, randomised to biofeedback-augmented vs dose-matched standard rehabilitation, with weight-bearing ratios and joint moments measured pre-op, 6 weeks and 26 weeks. *Results not retrieved* — [ClinicalTrials NCT01333189](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01333189)
- **Bade et al. movement-pattern biofeedback RCT after TKA, n=150**, published in *Arthritis Care & Research* 2025; primary outcome **peak knee extension moment during walking**. *Published findings not retrieved* — [ClinicalTrials NCT03325062](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03325062)

**GRF / gait retraining after ACLR**
- A **19-person randomised crossover** study of real-time biofeedback aimed at increasing peak vertical GRF found larger vGRF increases in the intervention group than sham, but the authors **urged caution because confidence intervals crossed zero**; they concluded the retraining "may improve vGRF profiles" with gains possibly persisting 2 weeks — [NSF PAR 10497448](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10497448)
- A 2025 randomised experiment, "Peak Vertical Ground Reaction Force Modifies Gait Biomechanics Bilaterally Following ACL Reconstruction," and an exploratory poster linking peak vGRF during retraining to patient-reported knee function — [UNC abstract record](https://our.unc.edu/abstract/sallis-preliminary-analysis-of-lower-extremity-loading-in-acl-reconstructed-individuals-following-a-gait-retraining-intervention-using-real-time-gait-biofeedback); [Kroll poster 2024](https://our.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/1148/gravity_forms/82-7a1e4eb4503c5d81d3ea6318e2d31ced/2024/04/Kroll-Poster-2024.pdf)

**Hip fracture (largest planned trial)**
- **COMFORT trial**, multicentre single-blind RCT, real-time visual weight-bearing feedback during a 30 m walk vs usual care in older adults with proximal femur fracture; **94 participants per group, target 186**; primary outcomes **maximum peak load and step duration**, spatiotemporal gait measured with an ambulatory biofeedback system (SensiStep). *Protocol only; results not retrieved* — [Trials 19, PMC5898035](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5898035/); [Trials journal article](https://trialsjournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13063-018-2612-9)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports a **load/GRF biofeedback feature** more strongly than it supports a gait-metrics dashboard. The compliance effect sizes are large and consistent (overload steps from ~33–50% down to ≤8.4%; peak force 330 N vs 600 N).
- The major evidential weakness is **population**: the clean RCTs are in healthy volunteers, and the patient studies are small or uncontrolled. Marketing or regulatory claims built on this literature should be framed around *weight-bearing accuracy/compliance*, which is directly evidenced, rather than around clinical outcomes, which are supported only by small heterogeneous trials.
- Pfeufer's 9-of-11 positive finding is useful supporting evidence but is a review of heterogeneous biofeedback modalities (not specifically GRF), so it should not be cited as evidence that GRF feedback improves TKA outcomes.

### Gaps
- No results were retrieved for the COMFORT RCT, the Christiansen n=26 TKA trial, or the Bade n=150 movement-training trial — three of the most directly relevant studies. Their outcomes remain unknown to these notes.
- No evidence was found on whether **symmetry** biofeedback (as distinct from absolute-load biofeedback) improves outcomes in post-surgical populations.
- No cost-effectiveness or clinician-acceptance evidence for biofeedback devices was found.

## Q6. Literature and regulatory guidance on presenting derived/estimated versus measured values, and communicating uncertainty to clinicians

### Takeaway
Regulatory guidance (FDA CDS) is the firmer ground: a software function that stays outside device regulation must, among other criteria, be **transparent about the basis for its recommendations** so the clinician can independently review them, and the preferred bases are clinical practice guidelines and published literature. By contrast, **there is essentially no empirical literature on how to display measurement uncertainty in a clinical gait dashboard** — the closest evidence is indirect (clinician dashboard preference studies and a sensor-reliability preprint).

### Cited Findings
**Regulatory**
- FDA issued a **revised Clinical Decision Support guidance on 6 January 2026**, superseding the **28 September 2022** final guidance. It is non-binding and refines interpretations with practical examples and material enforcement discretion — [Jones Day, Jan 2026](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/01/a-relaxing-2026-fda-updates-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance); [Mondaq summary](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/healthcare/1741104/fda-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance-refresh-clearer-rules-broader-innovation)
- Transparency expectations summarised from the guidance: **intended use and users stated clearly, inputs and data quality described, and methods, data sources, validation and limitations explained in plain language**; outputs should include patient-specific context so clinicians can review the basis for a recommendation. The required level of detail need only be **"sufficient for the intended HCP user to understand the basis of the recommendation"** — [Katten quickread](https://quickreads.ext.katten.com/post/102mghc/fda-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance-refresh-clearer-rules-broader); [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/01/a-relaxing-2026-fda-updates-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance)
- The 2022 final guidance includes specific recommendations on how CDS software can disclose the **"basis for the recommendations"** it presents to healthcare providers; **clinical practice guidelines** are described as the clearest and most easily understandable basis, with published literature also accepted — [Ropes & Gray, Oct 2022](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/newsroom/alerts/2022/October/Is-Your-Clinical-Decision-Support-Software-a-Medical-Device)
- Historical context: the 2017 draft made **transparency the key determinant of device status regardless of risk profile**, which industry objected to — [Ropes & Gray 2019](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/newsroom/alerts/2019/10/FDA-Overhauls-its-Draft-Guidance-on-Clinical-Decision-Support-CDS-Software); [MedTech Dive](https://medtechdive.com/news/industry-blasts-fda-clinical-decision-software-draft/527258)
- AMIA's critique of the transparency criterion: functionalities "based on a trained neural network, multivariate regressions, or fuzzy logic will be difficult, if not impossible, for clinicians or patients to readily inspect or evaluate" — [AMIA response to FDA draft guidance](https://brand.amia.org/m/3292e1a7d4d34526/original/AMIA-Response-to-FDA-Draft-Guidance-on-Clinical-and-Patient-Decision-Support-Software.pdf)

**Uncertainty and data quality communication**
- The *GaitVista* preprint frames the problem as one of **trust, not accuracy alone**: a clinician reading a change in knee motion needs to know whether it reflects the patient or the sensing conditions. Its design argument is that under fixed or globally tuned sensor fusion, environmental or wear changes "alter the measurement **without indicating that sensing quality has changed**," making a sensor-quality signal important for longitudinal comparison — [arXiv 2609.22619](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.22619)
- A Frontiers editorial raises measurement uncertainty of wearable physiological sensors as a systematically neglected issue; one cited gait study compared a commercial insole system with video ground truth in chronic stroke and noted **"individual-specific patterns make [it] challenging to detect gait events accurately,"** implying uncertainty varies by patient — [PMC11109362](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11109362/); [Frontiers in Digital Health editorial](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2024.1423281/full)
- Clinician dashboard preferences (CarePortal participatory design study, *JMIR Formative Research* 2023): clinicians preferred **aggregate information** (e.g. daily heart rate rather than continuous heart rate) and wanted to **see trends over a period**. The authors warn it is "important **not to burden clinicians with excessive data visualization**" while also not omitting important features derived from the continuous data stream. *Heart-rate domain, indirect evidence for gait* — [JMIR Formative Res 2023;7:e46866](https://formative.jmir.org/2023/1/e46866/PDF)
- Reporting-practice guidance from clinical gait analysis places **data-quality sections first**: a CP-focused report template (based on Baker, McGinley & Thomason 2010) puts the quality sections first "because they affect how the data should be read" — [Report template, Springer Medizin supplementary](https://www.springermedizin.de/resource/supplementary/12887_2020_2315_MOESM1_ESM.pdf/18337870)
- A clinical-scientist training competency for gait report writing explicitly requires **stating the limits of local protocols and of each data set**, and recommends alternative displays such as gait profile scores — [NSHCS curriculum competency SPE340c2](https://curriculumlibrary.nshcs.org.uk/stp/competency/SPE340c2/8/)

### Inferences
- The FDA framing maps cleanly onto a console design rule: a **measured** value (load from a calibrated cell) and a **derived/estimated** value (e.g. an estimated joint load inferred from GRF) are not equivalent for transparency purposes. Derived values need a stated method and limitation so the clinician can judge the basis; measured values need a stated accuracy and calibration state.
- Because CPGs are named as the clearest acceptable basis, a console should **cite the guideline or paper behind any threshold it draws on screen**, and should not draw a threshold line it cannot cite (this reinforces the Q3 conclusion to avoid invented symmetry thresholds).
- The GaitVista argument implies a console should expose a **data-quality / confidence indicator per session**, distinguishing "this patient changed" from "this measurement changed," particularly for any longitudinal trend view.
- The clinician-preference evidence (aggregate + trend, not continuous streams; do not overload) argues for a console default view of a few aggregated metrics with trend over sessions, with raw per-step detail available but not foregrounded.

### Gaps
- **No study was found that empirically tests how clinicians interpret uncertainty intervals, confidence bands or data-quality badges for gait data.** This is a real evidence vacuum; design choices here will be reasoned, not evidenced.
- The primary text of the January 2026 FDA CDS guidance was not read; all transparency wording above comes from law-firm commentary and the 2022 guidance. **The exact regulatory wording should be verified against the FDA document before being relied on.**
- No EU MDR / IEC 62304 / IEC 82304 or usability-standard (IEC 62366) guidance on value presentation was retrieved within budget.

## Q7. Common criticisms and pitfalls of instrumented gait analysis in routine clinical practice

### Takeaway
The documented criticisms are predominantly about **cost, equipment burden, protocol and analysis complexity, lack of standardisation, and the expertise/time needed to interpret results** — plus demonstrated **inter-observer variability in interpretation** of objectively measured data. Direct criticism of IGA's accuracy or of "too many numbers" is thinner in the peer-reviewed literature than one might expect.

### Cited Findings
- IGA "remains not well leveraged in clinical settings," with cited reasons being **cost, cumbersome equipment, complex protocols and data analysis, and differing training and preferences among clinical teams**; use by allied health staff such as physiotherapists and orthotists is described as **non-standardised and limited** — [PMC12398296 (narrative review, lower-limb amputation)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12398296/)
- IGA is described as **underused and variably understood by relevant clinicians** in cerebral palsy — [Pilot and Feasibility Studies 2024](https://pilotfeasibilitystudies.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s40814-024-01477-5.pdf)
- "In ordinary clinical settings, the availability of a fully equipped gait laboratory is infrequent" — [Frontiers in Medical Technology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medical-technology/articles/10.3389/fmedt.2022.901331/full)
- Why clinicians default to observation: "Observational gait analysis continues to be popular among clinicians due to its inherent **simplicity, availability, and low cost**," while remaining "highly observational and is hence **subjective and largely influenced by the observer's background and experience**" — [Frontiers in Medical Technology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medical-technology/articles/10.3389/fmedt.2022.901331/full)
- **Interpretation, not measurement, is the weak link.** In a CP study, physicians agreed much more on soft-tissue problems than bony ones, and agreement on osteotomy recommendations was low; the authors concluded that "gait analysis data are themselves objective, [but] this study demonstrates some **subjectivity in their interpretation**" — [Noonan, TRAMA course presentation](https://trama.deib.polimi.it/allegati/course_january_2008/presentation_Paolinelli.pdf)
- "Interpreting clinical gait data in children with CP is **time-consuming, requires extensive expertise and often lacks transparency**" — the motivation for a Delphi-derived set of look-up tables mapping gait features to likely impairments — [Front Hum Neurosci 2022;16:907565](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2022.907565/epub)
- A general lack of substantial evidence affirming the **credibility, consistency, sensitivity and specificity** of simpler clinical gait tools — [Frontiers in Medical Technology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medical-technology/articles/10.3389/fmedt.2022.901331/full)
- Open challenges flagged for wearables/AI replacements: **"standardization of clinical protocols and ensuring their feasibility"** — [Frontiers in Medical Technology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medical-technology/articles/10.3389/fmedt.2022.901331/full)
- **Presentation-specific criticism (vendor opinion, not peer-reviewed):** a report with inconsistent units or critical findings buried in dense tables can lower surgeons' confidence and lead them to deprioritise recommendations — [Vicon blog](https://www.vicon.com/resources/blog/why-your-motion-analysis-report-is-the-most-important-output-your-lab-produces/)
- Supporting (counter-) evidence that IGA does change decisions: IGA "has proven impacts on clinical decision-making for children with cerebral palsy," where single-event multilevel surgeries are planned after IGA — [Pilot and Feasibility Studies 2024](https://pilotfeasibilitystudies.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s40814-024-01477-5.pdf)
- Within this population specifically, the knee OA cadence finding is a concrete instance of a gait metric lacking prognostic value: lower cadence was **not** significantly associated with incident or worsening knee symptoms, pain with walking, or functional limitations in MOST — [Univ. of Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Association-of-Walking-Cadence-to-Changes/9984786445502771)
- And the TKA finding that changes in gait outcomes were **not associated with perceived body function or patient satisfaction** is a direct over-interpretation risk — [CIRRIS record](https://www.cirris.ulaval.ca/publications/walking-speed-and-maximal-knee-flexion-during-gait-after-total-knee-arthroplasty-minimal-clinically-important-improvement-is-not-determinable-patient-acceptable-symptom-state-is-potentially-useful)

### Inferences
- The documented failure mode is **interpretation burden**, not data scarcity. A console that adds more numbers without adding interpretation is adding to the known problem. The Delphi look-up-table and gait-profile-score work suggests the valued direction is **mapping measurements to impairments or to a single interpretable summary**, not more raw channels.
- Two findings in this population (cadence not prognostic in knee OA; gait change not linked to satisfaction after TKA) give concrete, citable grounds for *excluding* metrics from a console: a metric can be measurable, reliable and clinically meaningless.
- The "too many numbers" criticism is widely repeated but, in what was retrieved, is best supported by the indirect clinician-dashboard evidence (Q6) and vendor opinion rather than by a peer-reviewed study of gait report overload. This should be presented as a design heuristic with weak evidential backing, not as a finding.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found testing whether reducing the number of displayed gait metrics improves clinician decision quality or speed.
- No study was found on whether IGA changes *outcomes* (as opposed to decisions) in routine adult orthopaedic rehabilitation.
- No study was found quantifying over-interpretation (e.g. clinicians acting on changes smaller than the MDC).

## Q8. Which gait reports or dashboards are considered good practice? Are there published guidelines on clinical gait report content?

### Takeaway
There is **no published consensus checklist for clinical gait report content** that was retrievable here. The best-established structural guidance is the **Baker, McGinley & Thomason (2010) report template** (data-quality sections first, each impairment linked to its supporting gait features and exam findings) and institutional gait-lab report structures; APTA has a CPG+ on 3D instrumented gait analysis in cerebral palsy with best-practice statements on reporting, but it is members-only.

### Cited Findings
- The CP report template based on **Baker, McGinley & Thomason (2010)** puts the **quality sections first, because they affect how the data should be read**; each impairment is linked to its supporting gait features and exam findings, and the report is intended to be **read alongside the graphs with the features marked** — [Report template, Springer Medizin supplementary](https://www.springermedizin.de/resource/supplementary/12887_2020_2315_MOESM1_ESM.pdf/18337870)
- An NHS gait-lab report (Oxford University Hospitals) includes **patient history, clinical examination, interpretation of video, kinematic, kinetic, foot kinematic, plantar pressure and EMG data where applicable, then treatment options** (physiotherapy, orthoses, botulinum toxin, surgery). Results are interpreted in an **inter-professional meeting** — [OUH gait lab results page](https://ulive.ouh.nhs.uk/gait-lab/gait-analysis/results)
- A UK clinical-scientist training competency lists the report's core elements: **combining data from different sources, relating findings to pathology, and stating the limits of local protocols and of each data set**, and recommends **alternative displays such as gait profile scores** — [NSHCS curriculum competency SPE340c2](https://curriculumlibrary.nshcs.org.uk/stp/competency/SPE340c2/8/)
- **APTA CPG+ "Three-Dimensional Instrumented Gait Analysis for Children With Cerebral Palsy"** includes best-practice statements on lab instrumentation, staffing and **reporting practices**. *Full text is members-only and was not reviewable* — [APTA CPG page](https://www.apta.org/patient-care/evidence-based-practice-resources/cpgs/three-dimensional-instrumented-gait-analysis-children-with-cerebral-palsy-evidence-based-cpg)
- **Baker et al. (2016, *Eur J Phys Rehabil Med*)** review guidelines for running a clinical gait analysis service, noting "the methodology for properly conducting and interpreting the exam is of paramount relevance" — [Salford repository record](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1396147/gait-analysis-clinical-facts)
- A Delphi study produced **look-up tables linking gait features to likely impairments**, built specifically to make interpretation faster and more transparent — [Front Hum Neurosci 2022;16:907565](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2022.907565/epub)
- An IGI Global chapter exists specifically on "Reporting Clinical Gait Analysis Data" — [IRMA/IGI Global chapter 53651](https://irma-international.org/chapter/reporting-clinical-gait-analysis-data/53651/)
- Clinician dashboard design evidence (indirect, heart-rate domain): clinicians preferred **aggregates and trends over continuous streams**, and authors warn against **excessive data visualization** while cautioning against omitting important derived features — [JMIR Formative Res 2023;7:e46866](https://formative.jmir.org/2023/1/e46866/PDF)
- Vendor opinion on report presentation: inconsistent units and critical findings buried in dense tables lower surgeon confidence — [Vicon blog](https://www.vicon.com/resources/blog/why-your-motion-analysis-report-is-the-most-important-output-your-lab-produces/)

### Inferences
- Four transferable report principles emerge from the retrievable guidance, all of which are directly applicable to a console: (1) **data quality / measurement conditions stated before the data**; (2) **each finding linked to its supporting evidence** rather than presented bare; (3) **limits of the protocol and of each data set stated explicitly**; (4) **a single interpretable summary index offered alongside the detail** (the gait-profile-score pattern).
- All of the retrievable report guidance comes from the **paediatric cerebral palsy** gait-lab tradition. There is no equivalent reporting tradition for adult post-surgical or arthritis rehabilitation, which means a console in this space is designing into a vacuum and should borrow the CP structural principles rather than invent a format.
- The "read alongside the graphs with features marked" principle argues against a numbers-only console display and for annotating the waveform/trace where the finding occurs.

### Gaps
- **No consensus checklist or reporting standard for clinical gait reports was found.** The APTA CPG+ is the most likely source of formal best-practice statements and could not be accessed.
- No gait report or dashboard evaluated in a peer-reviewed usability or decision-quality study was found. "Considered good practice" rests on expert templates, not on tested designs.
- Nothing was found on reporting practice for adult orthopaedic rehabilitation gait data specifically.

## Q9. How does treadmill gait differ from overground gait, and how should the caveat be communicated?

### Takeaway
For **mean spatiotemporal values in healthy adults on level ground, treadmill and overground walking can be statistically equivalent**, but the literature disagrees on **variability**, and in **clinical and older populations** absolute values do shift (altered double support, stance time, stride length and cadence). The reusable message is that treadmill data are valid for **within-subject and between-condition comparison** but should not be read against overground normative values without caveat.

### Cited Findings
- **Healthy young adults, level ground (n=20, 2015):** "Mean values for each of the gait parameters were **statistically equivalent** between treadmill and overground walking (p > 0.05)." The authors nonetheless cautioned that variability differences mean "the **transferability of training from treadmill to overground walking may be impacted**" — [PMC5260636](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5260636)
- **Variability — direct conflict in the literature.** One study found variability (standard deviation) "was **not** systematically lower during treadmill walking compared to overground walking," while a review reports treadmill gait "is associated with **altered temporal organization and decreased variability** of spatiotemporal variables" — [PMC8930449](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8930449); [PMC5260636](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5260636)
- **Older adults:** treadmill walking has been reported to **increase double support and stance times** in healthy young and older adults, and to **reduce stride length and increase cadence in older adults**; other studies found no significant differences, and reviewers note differences "may also depend on the population studied" — [PMC8930449](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8930449)
- **Parkinson's disease:** stride length and cadence both differed between conditions — patients "significantly increased their step length and step height; and **reduced their cadence, step width and step width variability** on the treadmill." Critically, the **group difference between PD patients and controls was maintained on the treadmill**, both when speed was matched and with ±15% speed variation — [J Parkinsons Dis, 10.3233/JPD-130251](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3233/JPD-130251); [Overground vs treadmill in PD, eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1s3069c6)
- **Running:** treadmill and overground running are largely comparable, "[but] small but systematic differences have been reported, particularly in **sagittal-plane mechanics**" — [Journal of Human Kinetics](https://johk.pl/?p=3699)
- Related normative caveat (from Q3) that compounds the issue: published comfortable-speed norms "may not apply if gait is timed over short distances starting from a 'go' command or if the walk includes a turn" — [Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, Gait Speed](https://www.sralab.org/rehabilitation-measures/gait-speed)

### Inferences
- The PD finding (group differences preserved across conditions and across ±15% speed) is the strongest available argument that **treadmill measurement preserves clinically relevant contrasts**, and is the right citation for defending a treadmill console's validity for *comparison*.
- The honest caveat to surface is directional and specific rather than generic: treadmill walking tends to **increase double support and stance time** and, in older adults, **shorten stride length and raise cadence**. A console that displays these specific variables should carry the caveat; one that displays speed and load symmetry is less exposed.
- Because the treadmill **imposes** the speed, speed is not a free outcome variable in the way it is overground. A console should distinguish a **self-selected-speed protocol** (where speed is an outcome and comparable to walk-test norms) from a **fixed-speed protocol** (where speed is an input and the gait variables are the outcome). Mixing the two across sessions would make trends uninterpretable.
- A console should treat its own normative comparison cautiously: overground normative speed tables (Bohannon) are not strictly applicable to treadmill-imposed speeds.

### Gaps
- No study was found comparing treadmill vs overground gait **in post-surgical (TKA/THA/ACLR) or arthritis populations specifically** — all condition-specific evidence found was in Parkinson's disease, healthy adults and older adults.
- No study was found on treadmill vs overground **ground reaction force or loading symmetry** (as opposed to spatiotemporal parameters), which is the variable most relevant to a load-cell treadmill.
- No published guidance was found on how to word a treadmill caveat in a clinical report.

## Cross-cutting notes for the report writer

### Takeaway
Two conclusions recur across questions and are worth stating once: (a) the evidence base is **strongest for weight-bearing load feedback and weakest for symmetry and spatiotemporal metrics**, and (b) the **guideline-recommended clinical measures in this population are largely not treadmill-measurable**, which bounds what a console can honestly claim.

### Cited Findings
- Guideline-recommended measures after TKA are KOOS JR, 30-Second Sit-to-Stand and TUG, graded Best Practice on Insufficient evidence; no gait-assessment recommendation — [APTA TKA CPG, PMC7462050](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7462050)
- Unaided partial weight-bearing compliance after TKA was 0%–2%, with 84%–92% of patients overloading on every step — [PMC10439061](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10439061/)
- Biofeedback reduced overload steps to ≤8.4% in a randomised trial (healthy adults) — [Sensors 24(19):6443](https://mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/19/6443/htm)
- Changes in gait outcomes after TKA were not associated with perceived body function or patient satisfaction — [CIRRIS record](https://www.cirris.ulaval.ca/publications/walking-speed-and-maximal-knee-flexion-during-gait-after-total-knee-arthroplasty-minimal-clinically-important-improvement-is-not-determinable-patient-acceptable-symptom-state-is-potentially-useful)
- There is no accepted standard for assessing gait symmetry, which prevents cross-study comparison and the establishment of clinical decision criteria — [Patterson et al. 2010](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1644239)
- FDA CDS transparency expects the basis for a recommendation to be reviewable by the intended clinician user, with clinical practice guidelines named as the clearest basis — [Ropes & Gray 2022](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/newsroom/alerts/2022/October/Is-Your-Clinical-Decision-Support-Software-a-Medical-Device)

### Inferences
- A defensible display hierarchy follows directly from the evidence strength: **(1) load / weight-bearing against prescription (strong evidence, large effect, addresses a documented clinical failure); (2) gait speed with cited change thresholds (recognised measure, usable MCID/MDC); (3) step length, cadence, symmetry as trends without significance claims (no MCID/MDC exists); (4) estimated/derived joint-level values only with method and limitation stated (FDA transparency).**
- Candidates for *omission* on evidence grounds: cadence as a prognostic indicator in knee OA (not associated with outcomes in MOST), any symmetry threshold line (no citable threshold), and any "clinically significant change" badge on spatiotemporal variables.

### Gaps
- The whole set of notes is weakest on **cartilage repair** (no sources found) and on **total hip arthroplasty-specific** thresholds (pooled with TKA in the toolkits).
- The three most relevant RCTs (COMFORT, Christiansen TKA biofeedback, Bade movement training) have no retrieved results.
- No empirical evidence exists on uncertainty presentation for gait data; design decisions there cannot be evidence-led.
