# Kinetic (force-based) gait metrics derivable from the vertical ground reaction force waveform

Scope note carried through every section: everything below is restricted to the **vertical** GRF component (Fz), which is what a four-load-cell deck sums. Measures requiring the anteroposterior (braking/propulsion) or mediolateral components are flagged explicitly as **[NOT VERTICAL-ONLY]**.

---

## Q1. Standard landmarks of the walking vGRF waveform (F1 weight acceptance, F2 midstance trough, F3 push-off), typical BW values, and speed dependence

### Takeaway
The walking vGRF is a double-humped ("M-shaped") curve with three universally used discrete landmarks — F1 (first/weight-acceptance peak), F2 (midstance minimum) and F3 (second/push-off peak) — and in healthy adults at comfortable speed all three sit in the rough range 0.7–1.3 BW. F1 and F3 rise with walking speed and F2 falls, so the waveform "deepens" with speed and flattens with slowness or pathology; because stride-average vGRF must equal body weight, speed changes essentially redistribute force around that 1.0 BW baseline rather than raising the mean.

### Cited Findings
- The three-landmark naming convention F1 / F2 / F3 is used explicitly in the ACLR gait literature: **F1 = the first maximal vertical force at heel contact (weight acceptance), F2 = the minimal force between F1 and F3 (mid-stance trough), F3 = the second maximal vertical force at push-off** — [Fan et al., Orthop J Sports Med 2026 (PMC13198663)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- Equivalent alternative naming in the Parkinson's/insole literature: **initial contact (IC) peak "corresponded broadly to the heel strike and the phase of weight-acceptance"; mid-stance (MS) is "the point where vGRF is at its minimum"; terminal stance (TS) peak "aligned with terminal stance (TS) and toe-off."** — [vGRF in Parkinson's Disease, speed-matched (PMC10781249)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781249/)
- The curve is described as having "the passive (weight acceptance) peak and the active (push off) peak"; the vertical component across the whole sole "forms an M-shaped curve, with the first peak occurring soon after the heel strike and the second peak during push-off" — [Analysis and Interpretation of Ground Reaction Forces in Normal Gait, WSEAS 2009](https://www.wseas.us/e-library/transactions/systems/2009/29-755.pdf)
- Typical healthy adult magnitudes from that same (older, low-detail) review: **first peak ≈ 107% BW, second peak ≈ 105% BW** — [WSEAS 2009](https://www.wseas.us/e-library/transactions/systems/2009/29-755.pdf)
- Healthy control values at matched comfortable speed (~1.34 m/s), measured on a tandem-force-plate treadmill: **weight-acceptance peak 1.55 (0.12) BW right / 1.52 (0.12) BW left; push-off peak 1.16 (0.12) / 1.17 (0.10) BW** — [Knee OA GRF study (PMC5396017)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/). Note these F1 values are notably higher than most other reports; the same paper collected controls at a "top walking speed" of 2.00 m/s, so speed and treadmill-specific processing likely inflate F1.
- Healthy control values from barefoot overground walking at preferred speed (AMTI force plates): **Fz1 = 1.05 BW (SE 0.018), Fz2 (second peak) = 1.117 BW (SE 0.019)** — [THR gait and impulse study (PMC11608778)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- Healthy control values at preferred overground speed (~1.13–1.15 m/s) in the ACLR cohort's uninjured limbs: **F1 ≈ 100% BW, F2 ≈ 75% BW, F3 ≈ 104% BW** (lesser-loading group) and **F1 ≈ 80%, F2 ≈ 62%, F3 ≈ 84% BW** (greater-loading group's uninjured side, which was the compensating limb) — [Fan et al. (PMC13198663)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- Speed dependence of peak vertical force across walking→running: **"F_Z increased linearly during walking and running from 1.2 BW to approximately 2.5 BW at 6.0 m·s⁻¹"**, then plateaued during forward-lean sprinting; male and female subjects showed similar patterns — Keller et al., *Clinical Biomechanics* 11(5):253–259, 1996, as reported via [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0268003395000682)
- Keller et al. also found **slow jogging produced peak force and loading rate >50% higher than either walking or fast running**, attributed to a higher, less-controlled centre-of-gravity excursion — Keller et al. 1996, via [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0268003395000682). This matters directly for "slow running" rehab protocols.
- Walking F1 rises roughly linearly with speed: at speeds approaching the walk–run transition the **first peak averaged 13.6 N/kg (≈1.39 BW) and "increased linearly with increase of speed", while the second peak averaged 11.3 N/kg (≈1.15 BW) and decreased as the transition approached** — [ISB 2001 walk–run transition abstract](https://isbweb.org/images/conf/2001/Longabstracts/PDF/0000_0099/0039.pdf)
- F1 and F3 do not scale at the same rate with speed: across 0.8–1.4 m/s in 10 healthy adults, "the two vertical GRF peaks grow at different rates, and the first peak becomes larger", attributed to collision work growing faster than push-off work (collision work rose 0.06 → 0.62 J/kg while push-off work rose 0.19 → 0.54 J/kg across the modelled range; the two balance near **1.2–1.35 m/s**, i.e. the mechanically preferred walking speed) — [Powered Simplest Walking Model preprint, bioRxiv 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.07.16.603707v1.full) (preprint, not peer reviewed)
- F2 is the least speed- and age-sensitive landmark in some reports: in younger vs older adults at self-selected and fast speeds "there were significant differences in FZ1 and FZ3 between the two groups (P<0.01) while there was no significant difference regarding FZ2 (P>0.05)" — [Effects of age and walking speeds on vertical ground reaction force, as indexed on Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/125405308/Effects_of_age_and_walking_speeds_on_vertical_ground_reaction_force_in_younger_and_older_adults) (abstract-level only; full text returned HTTP 403)
- In children walking on a treadmill, "Fz1 and all the antero-posterior forces increased with the speed while Fz2 decreased, and Fz3 was not influenced by speed variation" — reported in search results for the age/speed literature, see [search trail above](https://www.academia.edu/125405308/Effects_of_age_and_walking_speeds_on_vertical_ground_reaction_force_in_younger_and_older_adults)
- The physical constraint that anchors all of this: **"the vertical GRF must equal (or be very close to) body weight over the complete stride"** — [Powered Simplest Walking Model preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.07.16.603707v1.full)
- Speed itself should be treated as a covariate, not a nuisance: in children, GRF patterns "were characterized predominantly by normalized speed of progression and not age" — Stansfield et al., *J Pediatr Orthop* 2001, via [Strathprints record](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/3718/)
- A large reference dataset exists for comparison: **GaitRec — 2,084 patients with musculoskeletal impairments (hip 450, knee 625, ankle 627, calcaneus 382) plus 211 healthy controls; 75,732 bilateral walking trials**, vertical + AP + ML GRF and COP, converted at 2000 Hz but released down-sampled to 250 Hz, force expressed as a multiple of body weight (divided by mass × g), time-normalised to 101 points over stance and low-pass filtered at 20 Hz — [GaitRec, Scientific Data (PMC7217853)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217853/)

### Inferences
- A defensible design-time normative band for healthy adults at comfortable overground speed (~1.2–1.4 m/s) is **F1 ≈ 1.00–1.20 BW, F2 ≈ 0.70–0.80 BW, F3 ≈ 1.05–1.20 BW**, built from the convergence of Fan et al. (100/75/104 %BW), PMC11608778 (1.05 / – / 1.12 BW) and WSEAS (107/–/105 %BW). Report F1/F2/F3 together with the measured speed; a single-number norm is not meaningful.
- Because F1 and F3 rise and F2 falls with speed, **peak magnitude alone cannot distinguish a slow healthy walker from a cautious patient**. Speed-matching, or speed as a regression covariate, is mandatory for any clinical threshold on F1/F2/F3.
- Keller's slow-jogging finding implies that for a rehab deck, the **transition from fast walking to slow jogging is the single largest step change in both peak force and loading rate** a patient will experience — more than further increases in running speed. That makes slow running the highest-risk, most worth-monitoring band.
- GaitRec's 20 Hz low-pass and 250 Hz release rate are adequate for F1/F2/F3 and impulse, but would destroy a heel-strike transient; treat landmark magnitudes as robust and transient-based measures as processing-dependent.

### Gaps
- **No single published normative table of F1/F2/F3 in BW against walking speed for healthy adults** was found despite several targeted queries. The Keller 1996 relation is the best speed→peak source but covers walking at 1.5–3.0 m/s (faster than typical clinical walking) and reports only "peak F_Z", not F1/F2/F3 separately. The 2024 bioRxiv preprint reports the speed trend but the numeric peak/trough values are in figures only.
- Full text of Keller et al. 1996 was not retrieved; the 1.2→2.5 BW figures and the 8–30 BW/s loading-rate range come from the ScienceDirect abstract and secondary citations and should be verified against the original before being published as norms.
- No normative value for F2 against speed in adults with confidence intervals.
- The PMC5396017 control F1 of 1.52–1.55 BW conflicts with every other source (~1.05–1.20 BW). I cannot resolve this; likely a treadmill/insole-processing or speed difference. Flagged rather than averaged in.

## Q2. Loading rate and impact peak: definitions, computation, and required sampling rate

### Takeaway
Loading rate is the slope of the rising limb of vGRF, but there are at least four non-interchangeable definitions that differ by up to 3× on the same data, and the two most-cited ones require an impact peak that is absent in 22–36% of running trials and is not a reliable feature of walking at all. Sampling rates used in the primary literature range 120 Hz (peaks only) to 2000 Hz (loading rate), but I found **no study that directly quantified the effect of sampling rate on loading-rate accuracy**.

### Cited Findings
- Four distinct methods, all defined as slopes of vGRF, were compared head-to-head on the same data: (1) **maximum (instantaneous) slope over the 20–80% range of vGRF magnitude up to the impact peak**; (2) **average slope over that same 20–80% range**; (3) **average slope from initial contact (IC) to the impact peak (IP)**; (4) **average slope over 3–12% of stance time** — method (4) is the only one that does not require an impact peak — [Vertical Loading Rate Is not Associated with Running Injury, Regardless of Calculation Method (PMC9288487)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/)
- Values from those four methods on identical data, in N/kg/s (least-squares means):

  | Method | 2.68 m/s | Preferred (~3.9 m/s) | 4.47 m/s |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Max slope, 20–80% to IP | 667.4 | 1036.0 | 1210.1 |
  | Avg slope, 20–80% to IP | 535.5 | 882.1 | 1043.4 |
  | Avg slope, IC to IP | 398.4 | 603.5 | 706.9 |
  | Avg slope, 3–12% stance | 412.2 | 635.3 | 739.2 |

  — [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/). Method × speed interacted significantly (p < 0.001); LR increased with speed for all four methods; the IC-to-IP and 3–12%-stance methods did not differ significantly from each other.
- The authors' explicit conclusion: LR values "must be interpreted in the context of both method and speed", so **thresholds from one method or speed do not transfer to another** — [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/). A second source states the same in stronger terms: "thresholds can only be compared to loading rate values calculated using the same calculation method and collected at the same running speed" — [ACSM commentary](https://acsm.org/vertical-loading-running-injury-predictor/)
- Additional definitional trap: some studies define the 20–80% window by **time to IP** rather than by **vGRF magnitude**; the authors state these are not interchangeable — [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/)
- **Impact peak definition:** "the local maximum in the vGRF before the peak vGRF" — [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/)
- **Impact peak is frequently absent**, even in running: collections with no identifiable IP occurred in **36.0% of trials at 2.68 m/s, 28.1% at preferred speed, 22.4% at 4.47 m/s**. The study's workaround was to estimate the IP location from that athlete's average IP location, or failing that at **30.79% of stance time to the active peak** — [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/)
- Signal-processing chain actually used for loading rate: **Bertec instrumented treadmill at 2000 Hz, bi-directional third-order Butterworth low-pass at 50 Hz cut-off, foot contact/toe-off thresholded at 50 N, ~20 strides averaged per limb per speed** — [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/)
- In the running VLR literature the alternative formulation is explicitly "the slope of the vertical force between 20 and 80% of the duration from initial foot contact to the impact peak", and "a steeper slope means a more rapid collision" — see [search trail](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/) and [Springer, Correspondence Between Values of Vertical Loading Rate and Oxygen Consumption](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-022-00491-2)
- **Units:** BW/s is the body-weight-normalised convention ("the units of body weight per second (bw/sec)"), with reported magnitudes such as "vertical instantaneous loading rate... decreased from 68.32 (14.20) to 46.77 (13.95) BW/s" in a running gait-retraining context — [Stryd metric description](https://blog.stryd.com/2022/12/09/impact-loading-rate-lower-body-stress-score/) (vendor source, treat as unit convention only, not as a norm). Note PMC9288487 instead reports N/kg/s; divide by 9.81 to convert N/kg/s → BW/s.
- Keller et al. reported walking-through-running loading rate spanning roughly **8 to 30 BW/s** across the speed range — Keller et al. 1996, via [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0268003395000682) (secondary citation, unverified against original)
- Walking-specific loading rate as used clinically: in the ACLR cohort, "loading rate: calculated from the initial half of stance", reported in **%BW/s** — [Fan et al. (PMC13198663)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/). Reported values: injured limb pre-op 34 ± 1 → 41 ± 2 at 1 year (both groups); uninjured pre-op 41 in the lesser-loading group.
- Sampling rates used across the primary literature for vGRF work: **2000 Hz** (Bertec treadmill, loading rate — [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/)); **2000 Hz converted, 250 Hz released** (GaitRec — [PMC7217853](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217853/)); **1000 Hz** (AMTI OR6 and Kistler 9281B plates — [PMC13198663](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/), [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)); **120 Hz** (Zebris instrumented treadmill, peaks and timing only — [PMC3766030](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3766030/)); **100 Hz** (tandem piezoelectric treadmill — [PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)); **50 Hz** (Pedar-X insoles — [PMC10781249](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781249/)); **20 Hz** (research insole for cumulative load — [Achilles cumulative load preprint, PMC12236876](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12236876/))
- General sampling theory, cited only as a principle: "to avoid aliasing, the signal must be sampled at least at twice the frequency of the maximum frequency component of the input signal" — [ScienceDirect topic page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/sampling-rate)

### Inferences
- **Practical sampling-rate recommendation, inferred from what the literature actually uses rather than from a direct study:** F1/F2/F3 magnitudes, landmark timing and impulse are reliably obtained at **100–250 Hz** (Zebris at 120 Hz achieved 2–3% SEM on peaks; GaitRec publishes at 250 Hz). Loading rate, and any attempt at an instantaneous/maximum slope, should use **≥1000 Hz, preferably 2000 Hz**, because the quantity being estimated is a derivative and the instantaneous variant samples the single steepest inter-sample interval — which is directly biased by sample spacing. A 20–50 Hz low-pass anti-alias/smoothing filter is standard; the filter cut-off has at least as much influence on the reported number as the raw rate.
- Because walking rarely presents a reliable, repeatable impact peak (and 22–36% of *running* trials lack one), **a four-load-cell rehab deck should prefer the "average slope over a fixed fraction of stance" family (method 4) or an F1/time-to-F1 average rate**, both of which are IP-free and therefore computable on every step. Any metric that silently estimates a missing IP location (as PMC9288487 had to) will produce uninterpretable clinical trends.
- The most defensible walking loading-rate definition for a device is **average vertical loading rate = F1 / t(F1)**, in BW/s, with F1 in BW and t measured from the 20–50 N contact threshold. This is consistent with Fan et al.'s "initial half of stance" framing and is the only version a clinician can reason about without knowing the processing chain. It must be published alongside its definition.
- A loading-rate number without (a) the method, (b) the speed, and (c) the filter cut-off is clinically meaningless; this is the single strongest, most repeated message in the loading-rate literature.

### Gaps
- **No study directly quantifying how sampling rate affects measured loading rate or impact peak magnitude** was found, despite targeted searching. PMC9288487 explicitly declines to analyse this ("the article does not analyze how sampling rate affects LR"). This is a real gap in the literature, not a search failure — the methodological work that exists addresses calculation method and filter choice instead.
- Fan et al.'s walking loading-rate values (34–41 %BW/s) are **dimensionally implausible** as stated: F1 ≈ 100 %BW reached in ~0.1–0.15 s implies ~700–1100 %BW/s. Either the normalisation is per-% -stance rather than per-second, or there is a reporting error. Do not use these as reference values.
- No normative walking loading rate in BW/s for healthy adults with CIs was found.
- No published MDC for loading rate.

## Q3. Vertical impulse: computation and clinical use

### Takeaway
Vertical impulse is the time integral of vGRF over stance (or a sub-phase), in BW·s, and its clinical selling point is that it captures magnitude *and* duration in one number — which makes it more sensitive than isolated peaks to the slow, cautious, prolonged-stance strategies that dominate post-surgical and osteoarthritic gait. It is the measure most directly computable from a vertical-only deck, and sub-phase impulses (loading response vs pre-swing) localise the deficit within stance.

### Cited Findings
- Core rationale: **"Impulse takes into account both the magnitude of loading and duration of stance phase of a limb"**, and "the use of impulse rather than solitary peaks in the analysis of ground reaction forces may be more sensitive in detecting significant abnormalities in gait" — [Analysis of vGRF variables by Foot scan in hemiplegic patients (PMC4101393)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4101393/) (abstract/search-level; the full text was behind a reCAPTCHA on repeated fetch attempts)
- **Explicit formulas** as implemented in a THR gait study: general form `I = ∫F dt = mv₂ − mv₁`; discrete **vertical** form `J = Σ(GRFᵢ − mg)·dt` (i.e. body weight is subtracted, giving a *net* vertical impulse); discrete horizontal form `J = Σ GRFᵢ·dt`. `dt = 1/(GRF sample rate) = 1/1000 s`. Impulse reported in **Ns/BW** — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- **Stance sub-phase windows** used for sub-phase impulse integration: loading response (LR) 0–16% of stance; mid-stance (MS) 16–50%; terminal stance (TS) 50–83%; pre-swing (PS) 83–100% — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- THR numeric vertical impulse values (Ns/BW), operated side vs control: **whole cycle 0.64 (SE 0.017) vs 0.698 (SE 0.015), p = 0.029; loading response 0.117 (SE 0.005) vs 0.146 (SE 0.004), p < 0.0001**; mid-stance 0.194 vs 0.208, terminal stance 0.200 vs 0.211, pre-swing 0.132 vs 0.138 (all n.s.) — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- THR non-operated side vs control: **whole cycle 0.657 (SE 0.016) vs 0.716 (SE 0.015), p = 0.019; pre-swing 0.114 (SE 0.004) vs 0.149 (SE 0.004), p < 0.0001**; loading response 0.127 vs 0.135 (n.s.) — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- Clinical reading of those numbers: the lower operated-side loading-response impulse and lower Fz2 were interpreted as "a reduced ability to control centre-of-mass descent and produce push-off force"; the authors recommended targeting posture control and balance in early and late stance, and hip extensor strength and hip extension range — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- Knee OA impulse values (affected / unaffected / control right-left), units reported as "BWN/s": **total impulse 0.49 (0.04) / 0.53 (0.04) / 0.46 (0.04) and 0.46 (0.04); weight-acceptance impulse 0.113 (0.014) / 0.120 (0.015) / 0.146 (0.010) and 0.142 (0.011); push-off impulse 0.091 (0.008) / 0.098 (0.007) / 0.110 (0.011) and 0.111 (0.009)** — [PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)
- In that OA cohort the **total impulse symmetry ratio was the best single discriminator of OA vs control at top walking speed: AUC 0.902, cut-off −3%, sensitivity 88%, specificity 95%** — [PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)
- Impulse behaves non-intuitively in slow pathological gait: in hemiplegia, "loading rate and push-off rate were significantly less on both affected and unaffected sides... Greater impulse and stance time were recorded in both sides of patients than the control group" — [PMC4101393](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4101393/)
- A joint-specific impulse-based dosing index exists: across 35 weight-bearing rehabilitation exercises and ADLs, "a loading index was computed via a weighted sum of loading peak and cumulative loading impulse for each exercise" (equal weights), and exercises were then tiered low/moderate/high — [Patellofemoral Joint Loading Progression (PMC10315869)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10315869/)

### Inferences
- **Two incompatible impulse conventions are in circulation** and must be disambiguated in any product spec: (a) **total/gross impulse** `∫F dt` over stance, which for steady walking ≈ mean force × stance time ≈ 0.6–0.7 BW·s per step at comfortable speed, and (b) **net impulse** `∫(F − BW) dt`, which integrates to ~0 over a full stride in steady gait and only carries information when evaluated over sub-phases. PMC11608778 states formula (b) but reports whole-cycle magnitudes of 0.64–0.72, which are consistent with (a), not (b) — so their stated formula and their reported numbers cannot both be right. Report gross impulse and say so.
- The PMC5396017 impulse unit "BWN/s" is almost certainly a typo for **BW·s**: 0.46–0.53 matches mean-force × stance-time at the fast speeds used. Impulse cannot have units of force per time.
- Because impulse rises with slow, long stance while peaks fall, **impulse and peak force move in opposite directions in cautious gait**. A rehab dashboard that shows only impulse will make a guarding patient look *more* loaded, not less. Report impulse alongside stance time and peak force, or normalise impulse by stance time (which recovers mean force).
- Vertical impulse and its loading-response / pre-swing sub-phase decomposition are **fully computable from a four-load-cell vertical-only deck** and are the highest-value kinetic output of such a device: they need only Fz(t) and contact/toe-off events.
- The patellofemoral loading index (peak + cumulative impulse, equally weighted) is a usable template for a vertical-only "load dose" score, since both of its inputs are vertical-derivable.

### Gaps
- No **minimal detectable change** value for walking vertical impulse was found in any source.
- No large-sample normative table for vertical impulse by speed; the control values available (0.46 and 0.70 BW·s) come from two small studies at different speeds on different hardware and disagree by ~50%, which is almost certainly a speed/convention artefact rather than a real difference.
- The hemiplegic-gait paper (PMC4101393), which appears to be the most explicit source for walking-specific loading rate / push-off rate / impulse definitions, could not be retrieved in full (reCAPTCHA). Its definitions should be obtained before finalising a metric spec.

## Q4. "Weight acceptance" and how it is quantified; trough-to-peak and F1/F2 ratios

### Takeaway
"Weight acceptance" is operationalised three ways: as the **F1 peak magnitude** (BW), as the **loading rate** into F1 (BW/s), and as the **loading-response impulse** (BW·s over the first ~16% of stance) — and the loading-response impulse was the single most significantly impaired variable on the operated side after THA. The F2/F1 "trough-to-peak" relationship is used as a **waveform flattening** construct rather than as a standard named ratio: a flattened M (F1↓, F2↑, F3↓) is the canonical pathological signature.

### Cited Findings
- F1 as the weight-acceptance quantifier is the dominant convention: "F1: first maximal vertical force at heel contact (weight acceptance peak)" — [Fan et al. (PMC13198663)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/); labelled "PIF, Peak Impact Force" in the TKA literature and "weight acceptance peak" in the OA literature — [TKA pilot RCT (PMC11539413)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539413/), [PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)
- Weight acceptance as an impulse over a defined window: loading response = **0–16% of stance**, and this was where the operated THR limb differed most from controls (0.117 vs 0.146 Ns/BW, **p < 0.0001**, the largest effect in the study) — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- Weight-acceptance impulse in knee OA: affected 0.113 (0.014) vs control 0.146 (0.010) — a ~23% deficit — [PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)
- **Waveform flattening** is an explicitly studied construct in knee OA: a study titled *"Vertical Ground Reaction Forces Waveform Flattening during Gait in Women with Knee Osteoarthritis"* analysed vGRF peaks, the **minimum vGRF**, and knee range of motion in 12 women with knee OA vs 12 age-matched controls, and interpreted "the flattened vGRF waveform and reduced knee RoM as an adaptive but potentially maladaptive strategy". Reported OA values include **vGRF peak 1 = 1.109 (0.05)** (right leg) and **minimum vGRF = 0.87 (0.04)** (left leg). Gait speed did not differ between groups — [Bouchouras et al., arXiv 2510.16942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16942)
- The companion evidence that F2 rises toward 1.0 BW in OA: knee OA affected-limb weight-acceptance peak 1.18 (0.14) BW vs control 1.55 (0.12) BW and push-off peak 0.95 (0.07) vs 1.16 (0.12) BW — i.e. both humps collapse toward the 1.0 BW baseline — [PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)
- F2 in the ACLR cohort tracked F1 and F3 closely: injured-limb F2 was 61 %BW (lesser-loading) vs 78 %BW (greater-loading) pre-op, both converging to ~68 %BW at one year — [Fan et al. (PMC13198663)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- A timing-based trough metric exists in the modelling literature: a **"Trough Deficit Index"** for the timing of the midstance trough — [bioRxiv 2026 preprint, Vertical Ground Reaction Force Morphology...](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.03.09.710627v1.full) (preprint, not peer reviewed; definition not retrieved in detail)
- A low-quality but explicit statement of the pattern: typical comfortable-walking peaks ~105–125% BW with a mid-stance valley ~70–85% BW, and "slow walking flattens both peaks" — [mechcodex.com reference page](https://mechcodex.com/learn/dynamics-vibration/ground-reaction-force-in-gait) (non-peer-reviewed web page with untraceable figures; included only because it states the flattening relationship plainly)

### Inferences
- **No standard named "F1/F2 ratio" or "trough-to-peak ratio" metric exists in the clinical literature** under those names. The construct is real and well-evidenced (flattening) but is reported as separate F1, F2, F3 values plus a qualitative "flattened" description. A device could legitimately define `flattening index = F2 / ((F1 + F3)/2)` or `trough depth = ((F1+F3)/2 − F2)`, where **a value approaching 1.0 (or 0 trough depth) indicates a flat, pathological waveform and larger trough depth indicates a healthy, speed-appropriate M-shape** — but this would be a new metric, and the normative/speed-dependence work to support it does not yet exist. It must be presented as a derived index, not a validated clinical measure.
- Because trough depth is strongly speed-dependent (F1↑, F2↓ with speed), any flattening index is really measuring "speed-appropriateness of the waveform" and must be interpreted against measured speed. This is both its weakness (confounded) and its potential strength (it may detect a patient walking *fast but flat*, i.e. compensating).
- **Loading-response impulse (0–16% stance) is the best-evidenced single weight-acceptance metric for post-surgical populations** — it carried the largest effect size in the THA study and a ~23% deficit in knee OA — and is vertical-only computable. It should be preferred over F1 alone.

### Gaps
- No published normative values, speed-regression, reliability or MDC for any F2/F1 or trough-depth ratio.
- The arXiv flattening paper's actual flattening metric and formula could not be extracted: the PDF text was in compressed streams and the arXiv abstract page was partly garbled. Only the abstract-level numbers above are reliable, and even some of those contain corrupted p-values per the fetch. n = 12 per group.

## Q5. Normalisation conventions: body weight, body mass, BW × height

### Takeaway
For **forces** the near-universal convention is division by body weight (BW = mass × g), giving a dimensionless multiple of BW; for **impulses** it is BW·s (or BW × leg-length-scaled time if full dimensionless scaling is wanted); **BW × height** is the convention for *joint moments*, not for GRF, and should not be applied to force magnitudes. Hof's (1996) dimensionless scaling is the theoretically principled alternative, and normalised **speed** (v / √(g·height), the square root of the Froude number) matters more for comparability than any force normalisation.

### Cited Findings
- GRF normalisation in practice: force is "expressed as a multiple of body weight by dividing by body mass times gravitational acceleration" — [GaitRec (PMC7217853)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217853/)
- The same, stated as a formula: `norm %vGRF = (vGRF / BW) × 100`, with "BW converted in Newton" — [PMC10781249](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781249/)
- Mixed conventions coexist within single papers: GRF "normalized to body mass or body weight (N/kg or N/BW)", with impulse in **Ns/BW** — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- Loading rate is reported in **N/kg/s** in some running work and **BW/s** in others; conversion is a factor of g = 9.81 — [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/) vs [Stryd](https://blog.stryd.com/2022/12/09/impact-loading-rate-lower-body-stress-score/)
- Average vGRF normalised "by dividing by body weight (dimensionless, body-weight units)" — [Weight-Bearing Asymmetry in Knee OA (PMC2948025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2948025/)
- **Hof (1996), "Scaling gait data to body size", *Gait & Posture***, "proposed a dimensionless normalisation scheme based on the fact that the Earth's gravitational force is the most relevant external force to terrestrial locomotion". Under this scheme impulses are "normalized to each subject's body weight, leg length and standard gravity" — [search trail / citing papers](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966636217301273)
- The stated rationale for simple ratio normalisation of GRF is thin: reviews note "the only rationale on this normalization procedure being that this leaves the normalized variable dimensionless", and that "ratio normalisation, which dividing the force by weight, is recommended", though it "removes much of the explained variance when forces are related to body mass" — [Normalization of Ground Reaction Forces, Joint Moments, and Free Moments in Human Locomotion (ResearchGate record)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235370560_Normalization_of_Ground_Reaction_Forces_Joint_Moments_and_Free_Moments_in_Human_Locomotion)
- **Published critique of dimensionless normalisation:** a 2017 *Gait & Posture* paper argues dimensionless normalisation "may not fully remove the correlation between the ST variables and leg length" and "can introduce spurious correlations", while noting the method still has uses — [Undesirable properties of the dimensionless normalisation for spatio-temporal variables](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966636217301273)
- **Normalised speed** is the dominant comparability variable: Stansfield et al. defined normalised speed as "speed divided by the square root of height times gravity" (= √Froude) across 1,040 trials in a 7-year longitudinal cohort, concluding GRF patterns "were characterized predominantly by normalized speed of progression and not age" — [Strathprints record](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/3718/)
- Time normalisation convention for waveform comparison: "time-normalized to 101 points (100% stance)" with low-pass filtering at 20 Hz — [GaitRec (PMC7217853)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217853/)
- Speed is routinely entered as a statistical covariate rather than normalised away: "Walking speed was included as a covariate for the loadsol outcomes" — [PMC11539413](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539413/)

### Inferences
- **Recommended conventions for a vertical-only rehab deck**, consistent with the bulk of the cited literature: force in **BW** (or %BW); loading rate in **BW/s**; impulse in **BW·s**; waveform time-normalised to 101 points over stance for shape comparison, but *also* reported in absolute seconds because stance time is itself a clinical variable. Always publish the measured speed next to every value.
- **BW × height does not appear anywhere in the retrieved GRF literature as a force normalisation** — it is the joint-moment convention (%BW·ht). Using it for GRF would be a category error. If leg length / height is to enter, the principled route is Hof's full dimensionless scheme (force/BW, time × √(g/leg length)), not an ad-hoc BW·ht divisor.
- Dividing by BW and *also* speed-matching or covarying for speed is doing most of the work; the Stansfield result implies that if you can only control for one thing, control for **normalised speed**, not body size.
- Because GRF-as-multiple-of-BW is already dimensionless and intuitive to clinicians ("1.2 times your body weight"), and the dimensionless-normalisation critique above shows no clean benefit for spatiotemporal variables, the simple BW ratio is the right default for a clinical product.

### Gaps
- The original Hof (1996) full text was not retrieved; the leg-length/√g scaling for impulse is reported via citing papers only. The exact dimensionless impulse expression should be verified against the original *Gait & Posture* article.
- No source found that empirically compares BW-ratio vs Hof-dimensionless normalisation **for GRF force magnitudes specifically** (the 2017 critique addresses spatiotemporal variables).

## Q6. Kinetic asymmetry measures: formulas and reported abnormality thresholds

### Takeaway
Three distinct formula families are in use and they are **not interchangeable** — a simple ratio LSI (surgical/non-surgical × 100, abnormal outside 90–110%), a signed/absolute symmetry index normalised by the limb mean (ASI = 2|L−R|/(L+R) × 100, 0 = perfect symmetry), and raw limb-difference comparisons. Reported thresholds: **LSI outside 90–110%** is the most commonly cited clinical cutoff; healthy-control ASI for weight-bearing is ~9% and knee-OA ~18%; and a vertical **impulse** symmetry ratio with a −3% cut-off achieved AUC 0.902 for distinguishing knee OA from controls.

### Cited Findings
- **LSI (ratio form):** `LSI = |Surgical / Non-Surgical| × 100`; values below 100% indicate less loading of the surgical limb. "An Limb Symmetry Index of less than 90% or greater than 110% is considered to be clinically meaningful and indicative of asymmetric loading." — [TKA pilot RCT (PMC11539413)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539413/). The paper gives **no rationale** for the 90/110 cutoffs beyond citing four prior LSI references.
- **ASI (absolute symmetry index):** `ASI = 2 × |(vGRF_L − vGRF_R) / (vGRF_L + vGRF_R)| × 100%`, where ASI = 0 is perfect symmetry; chosen for control groups because no limb is "affected" in healthy adults. The paired **loading symmetry ratio** for the patient group was `vGRF_affected / vGRF_non-affected` — [PMC2948025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2948025/)
- **Normative ASI for weight-bearing vGRF** (Five Times Sit-to-Stand): **knee OA 18.0 ± 14.4 vs healthy control 9.0 ± 6.9, p = 0.015**. In the same cohort, knee extensor strength ASI was 31.8 ± 23.2 (OA) vs 8.3 ± 7.8 (control), p < 0.001 — [PMC2948025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2948025/)
- Loading symmetry ratio in knee OA: **0.87 (0.15)** overall; **0.77 (0.13)** in those who needed their arms to rise, **0.93 (0.14)** in those who did not. **42 of 50 (84%)** OA participants loaded the affected limb less — [PMC2948025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2948025/)
- **TKA LSI normative/baseline values** (loadsol in-shoe, 10 m walk, three trials averaged, N = 35): **Peak Impact Force LSI 98.8 (14.4)%; Peak Pushoff Force LSI 101.4 (11.6)%**. Neither changed significantly with the intervention at 3 or 6 months (PIF LSI p = 0.296 / 0.094; PPF LSI p = 0.196 / 0.418) — [PMC11539413](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539413/)
- **Pooled TKA meta-analytic LSI:** across seven studies of sit-to-stand, "for the peak vertical ground reaction forces, the pooled limb symmetry index was 0.96 (CI₉₅% = [0.93, 0.99])", with the uninvolved limb carrying more load; "asymmetries in limb/knee loading persist beyond the one-year post-operative period following total knee arthroplasty, potentially contributing to degenerative changes for the uninvolved limb" — [Inter-limb kinetic asymmetries during sit-to-stand after unilateral TKA, systematic review and meta-analysis (PubMed 37774467)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37774467/)
- **Impulse symmetry with a validated cut-off:** in knee OA at top walking speed, "total impulse symmetry ratio was the best discriminator (AUC 0.902; cut-off −3%; sensitivity 88%; specificity 95%)" — [PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)
- **Binary limb-comparison grouping** (no index): ACLR patients were split by whether "vGRF F1 on the injured side was less than that on the uninjured side" (lesser-loading, n = 27) vs at-or-above (greater-loading, n = 28) — [Fan et al. (PMC13198663)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- **Strength LSI formula** for cross-reference, from the same ACLR paper: `(maximal torque injured / maximal torque uninjured) × 100%`. Reported values: greater-loading group 66% ± 6% pre-op → 87% ± 4% at 1 year (p = .001); lesser-loading group 67% ± 4% → 75% ± 3% (p = .11); between-group difference at 1 year p = .03, Cohen d = 3.39 — [Fan et al. (PMC13198663)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- Explicit warning about cross-study comparison: "LSI definitions vary, with some using a ratio of surgical to non-surgical limb and others a percent-difference formula. Check which formula a given study uses before comparing values across studies." — synthesis stated in the [TKA/OA search trail](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539413/)
- Statistical note worth copying: the TKA RCT applied **log transformations to the PIF and PPF LSI outcomes** — [PMC11539413](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539413/). Ratio-form LSIs are multiplicatively distributed and bounded below at 0, so this is the correct handling.
- Jump-landing asymmetry magnitudes for scale (not walking): peak vGRFs were **11.9% greater on the uninvolved limb during the first landing** of a drop vertical jump in ACLR athletes, but only **2.4% greater on the second landing** — [PubMed 37556873](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37556873/)

### Inferences
- **Formula choice materially changes the number.** A limb pair of 0.90 / 1.00 BW gives LSI = 90% (at the abnormality threshold), ASI = 10.5%, and percent-difference = 10%. A device should report **one** named index with its formula printed, and should prefer the **ratio LSI (surgical/non-surgical × 100)** because the 90–110% threshold is the only cutoff with broad clinical circulation.
- The 90–110% threshold has **weak primary justification** — the one paper that states it offers no derivation. It is a convention, not a validated diagnostic cutoff, and should be presented to clinicians as such.
- The **impulse symmetry ratio is better-validated than peak symmetry** for detecting pathology: AUC 0.902 with explicit sensitivity/specificity (PMC5396017) versus no reported diagnostic accuracy for peak LSI anywhere in the retrieved literature. This is a strong argument for an impulse-based symmetry metric on a vertical-only deck.
- Healthy-control asymmetry is **not zero**: ASI ≈ 9% in controls and TKA group-mean LSIs of 98.8% / 101.4% with SDs of 12–14% mean that **an individual's LSI of 90% or 110% is within one SD of a post-TKA group mean**. Individual-level interpretation of a single session's LSI is therefore fragile; trend over sessions is the defensible use.
- Peak-force LSI appears **insensitive to intervention** in walking after TKA (unchanged across a 6-month RCT, PMC11539413) while remaining abnormal in meta-analysis during sit-to-stand (LSI 0.96, PubMed 37774467). The inference is that **walking is too easy a task to expose TKA loading asymmetry**; higher-demand tasks (sit-to-stand, stairs, single-leg) or impulse/loading-rate rather than peak are needed. This has direct implications for what a walking-focused deck can and cannot detect.

### Gaps
- No MDC or SEM reported for any of the LSI/ASI measures in the retrieved sources (PMC11539413 explicitly reports none for the loadsol or LSI).
- No source derives or validates the 90–110% LSI threshold; the primary citations behind it were not retrievable.
- No normative LSI distribution for **walking** vGRF peaks in a healthy adult sample with CIs (the ASI 9% control figure is from sit-to-stand, not walking).

## Q7. Use in post-surgical rehabilitation (ACLR, TKA, THA) and osteoarthritis: what they predict or track

### Takeaway
vGRF asymmetry is best evidenced as a **pre-operative prognostic marker in ACLR** (lesser pre-op loading of the injured limb predicted worse quadriceps strength symmetry and persistent cocontraction a year later, with a large effect) and as a **persistent-deficit tracker in TKA/THA** (asymmetry outlasts one year post-op, implicating the contralateral limb's degeneration risk). In knee OA, vGRF deficits co-occur with reduced walking speed and waveform flattening, and the impulse symmetry ratio is the strongest single discriminator found.

### Cited Findings

**ACL reconstruction**
- Design: 55 completers (27 lesser-loading, 28 greater-loading by pre-op F1 comparison), barefoot overground walking at preferred speed, two AMTI OR6 force plates at 1000 Hz, three trials averaged, Vicon MX-T20 at 100 Hz — [Fan et al. (PMC13198663)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- Pre-op vGRF (%BW, mean ± SD), injured / uninjured: **lesser-loading group F1 81±2 / 100±2, F2 61±2 / 75±2, F3 84±2 / 104±2; greater-loading group F1 96±2 / 80±2, F2 78±2 / 62±2, F3 97±2 / 84±2.** All three between-group pre-op p = .001 — [Fan et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- At 1 year, **vGRF converged between groups and between limbs**, while differences in knee muscle strength, neuromuscular activity and walking speed persisted — [Fan et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- Prognostic value: knee extensor symmetry index rose 66%→87% (p = .001) in the greater-loading group but only 67%→75% (p = .11) in the lesser-loading group; between-group difference at 1 year **p = .03, Cohen d = 3.39**. Cocontraction index at 1 year (injured side) **0.12 (lesser) vs 0.08 (greater), p = .049**; knee flexor strength 107% vs 93% BW, p = .047. **KOOS showed no between-group differences** at either timepoint — [Fan et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- Authors' conclusion: "preoperative vGRF asymmetry is a clinically feasible indicator of neuromuscular recovery potential" — [Fan et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198663/)
- Return-to-sport association: individuals who returned to sport after ACLR "exhibited significantly lower gait asymmetry and superior functional hop performance than those who did not", with vGRF asymmetry among the strongest predictors — [Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2026.1762965/full)
- A cross-sectional study of 28 males found "unilateral ACLR creates asymmetry in vGRF and muscle activities in different phases of the gait cycle" — [PubMed 31598766](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31598766/). **Caution: this paper carries an Expression of Concern** and should not be relied on.
- Psychological mediator: kinesiophobia is associated with gait asymmetry after ACLR — [PMC8004266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8004266/)

**Total knee arthroplasty**
- Pooled sit-to-stand peak vGRF **LSI 0.96 (95% CI 0.93–0.99)** across seven studies; "asymmetries in limb/knee loading persist beyond the one-year post-operative period... potentially contributing to degenerative changes for the uninvolved limb" — [PubMed 37774467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37774467/)
- Walking peak-force LSI did not respond to a 6-month activity/symmetry intervention (see Q6) — [PMC11539413](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11539413/)
- Concurrent force feedback during leg press and squat produced within-session improvement that **did not retain**: "A reduction in the mean load symmetry index following concurrent feedback training suggests improved use of the surgical limb during both leg press and squat exercises during the same therapy session but the more symmetric loading pattern was not retained one week later" — [IJSPT, Concurrent Force Feedback on Load Symmetry in TKA Patients](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/84312-concurrent-force-feedback-on-load-symmetry-in-total-knee-arthroplasty-patients)
- Weight-bearing asymmetry during sit-to-stand after TKA relates to impairment and functional mobility — [Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation (S0003-9993(11)00331-5)](https://www.archives-pmr.org/article/S0003-9993(11)00331-5/abstract)

**Total hip arthroplasty / replacement**
- Operated-side **Fz1 0.993 (SE 0.024) vs non-operated 1.087 (SE 0.025) N/BW, p = 0.006**; **Fz2 operated 1.022 (SE 0.025) vs control 1.117 (SE 0.019), p = 0.022**; pooled THR Fz2 1.04 vs 1.12 in controls, p = 0.017, with Fz1 not significantly different pooled — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- Loading-response impulse operated 0.117 vs control 0.146 Ns/BW (**p < 0.0001**); **non-operated** pre-swing impulse 0.114 vs control 0.149 (**p < 0.0001**) — i.e. both limbs are abnormal, in different stance sub-phases — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- Clinical reading: slower walking attributed to "the decreased impulse in loading response phase, the decreased 2nd peak of GRF and the decreased pre-swing impulse in vertical direction in operative side"; the operated side had "better push off but worse braking functions" **[the braking claim requires the AP component — NOT VERTICAL-ONLY]** — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/). Limitations: 10 THR vs 10 controls, retrospective, unequal sex/height distributions.
- In hip replacement patients monitored with insoles, "patients improved upon their ipsilateral loading and WBS deficits observed within a week post-surgery across all investigated ADLs" — [search trail, hip replacement insole study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39943463/)

**Knee osteoarthritis**
- Both vGRF humps collapse toward 1.0 BW: affected weight-acceptance peak 1.18 (0.14) vs control 1.55 (0.12) BW; push-off peak 0.95 (0.07) vs 1.16 (0.12) BW; at speed-matched comparison (OA top speed 1.37 m/s vs control preferred 1.34 m/s) the OA affected limb still had lower push-off and total impulse. Mean Kellgren-Lawrence total score 2.5 (SD 1.1), grade 2 most common. 100 Hz tandem piezoelectric treadmill, 10 s per trial — [PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/). Internal inconsistency: push-off force reported 22% lower in Results but 18% lower in the Abstract.
- OA patients were markedly slower at top speed (**1.37 vs 2.00 m/s**) and "asymmetry differences were more pronounced at top speed" — [PMC5396017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5396017/)
- Weight-bearing asymmetry in knee OA correlates with impairment and function (Pearson r with the vGRF symmetry ratio): **FTSST time −0.44 (−0.64 to −0.18); Stair Climb Test time −0.39 (−0.60 to −0.12); knee extensor torque ratio +0.37 (0.10 to 0.59); 6-Minute Walk distance +0.29 (0.01 to 0.52); knee extension ROM ratio −0.16 (n.s.)**. Knee pain during FTSST 3.0 ± 2.74 on a 0–10 NPRS — [PMC2948025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2948025/)
- Waveform flattening in knee OA is accompanied by reduced knee range of motion, interpreted as an adaptive but potentially maladaptive strategy — [arXiv 2510.16942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16942)

**Partial weight-bearing compliance (all post-surgical)**
- 50 TKA patients: compliance with the prescribed PWB limit was **0% on day one and 2% before discharge; 84% and 90% of patients (two measurement points) overloaded the affected limb on every step**. The authors note "the concept of partial weight-bearing has been questioned over the last years" — [Efficacy of post-operative partial weight-bearing after TKA (PMC10439061)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10439061/)
- 30 TKA patients with a biofeedback insole: "partial weight bearing with a correct gait in the post-operative period is not obtainable without a measuring system" — [Effect of an Innovative Biofeedback Insole on Patient Rehabilitation after TKA, Applied Sciences 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/5/2456)
- Acetabular fracture outpatients with a 20 kg limit: "despite regular physical therapy training compliance to the generally accepted weight-bearing limits was low" — [New Method for Monitoring Partial Weight Bearing (ResearchGate record)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/305399365_New_Method_for_Monitoring_Partial_Weight_Bearing_PWB_of_Outpatients_with_an_Independent_Insole_Sensor_System)
- Smart-insole PWB system described by its authors as "a valid and reliable rehabilitation tool for accurately measuring weight bearing loads", with real-time feedback "effective in PWB compliance" — [PubMed 40280798](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40280798/) (author's own device; verify independently)
- An ongoing RCT of tactile biofeedback after lower-limb surgery (34 enrolled; femoral/pelvic/hip fractures, hip and knee arthroplasty, ankle fractures) has not reported results — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06989554](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06989554)
- 84 patients with isolated ankle fractures were monitored continuously for six weeks with pressure-sensitive insoles to study orthosis use and load frequency under continuous biofeedback — [PubMed 39943463](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39943463/)

### Inferences
- The **ACLR pre-operative finding is the strongest single clinical argument for a vertical-force deck**: it needs only F1/F2/F3 at preferred walking speed, uses a trivially simple binary limb comparison, and predicts a year-out neuromuscular outcome with d = 3.39. It is also a *pre*-surgical measurement, which is where a clinic-floor device is easiest to deploy.
- A consistent and clinically important pattern across THA and TKA: **the contralateral "healthy" limb is also abnormal** (THA non-operated pre-swing impulse p < 0.0001; TKA meta-analysis implicating uninvolved-limb degeneration). A symmetry index alone will miss bilateral deficits entirely — absolute per-limb values against speed-matched norms are needed alongside it.
- PWB prescription is effectively **not achievable without measurement** (0–2% compliance unaided), and this is the best-evidenced clinical need a load-measuring deck addresses. The counter-evidence is equally important: the one retrieved retention study (IJSPT) found feedback-driven symmetry gains did not persist one week later, so the value proposition is *monitoring and in-session guidance*, not durable motor relearning.
- Because knee OA patients walk much more slowly and their deficits widen at higher speeds (PMC5396017), a deck that can capture a **fast/top walking speed trial** will be more sensitive than one restricted to preferred speed.

### Gaps
- No large multicentre trial linking insole/deck-measured weight-bearing feedback to clinical outcomes; the retrieved PWB studies are small single-centre studies, several from device developers.
- No retrieved study reports vGRF measures **predicting** TKA or THA outcomes (as opposed to tracking/describing them). The ACLR prognostic evidence has no TKA/THA equivalent in what I found.
- No ACLR vGRF asymmetry threshold linked to re-injury risk was retrieved; a systematic review on vGRF asymmetry as a re-injury risk factor was cited in search results but not retrieved.
- The THA study's Table 9 within-group values (operative LR 0.031 vs non-operative 0.146, and control values 0.299/0.421) are internally inconsistent with its own Tables 7–8 and the paper offers no explanation — do not use Table 9 numbers.

## Q8. Published normative values and minimal detectable change

### Takeaway
MDC₉₅ for **peak vGRF** in walking is small and well-characterised at roughly **2–4% of the mean (±43 to ±66 N, or ≈±0.05–0.10 BW)** for within- and between-day repeat testing in adults, with landmark **timing** MDC of ±2–3% of stance; kinetic parameters overall sit at **4–20% MDC₉₅**. There are **no published MDC figures for vertical impulse, loading rate, or any symmetry index** in the sources retrieved — a significant gap for any product claiming to track change.

### Cited Findings
- **Adult instrumented-treadmill reliability (n = 33, Zebris split-belt, 120 Hz, 30 s captures averaging 52 ± 5 steps).** The paper reports SEM and the 95% repeatability coefficient RC₉₅%, which is mathematically identical to MDC₉₅ — [PMC3766030](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3766030/):

  | Parameter | Within-day SEM | Within-day RC₉₅ | Between-day SEM | Between-day RC₉₅ |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | First peak, left (N) | 17 N (2%) | ±47 N | 19–20 N (3%) | ±54 to ±55 N |
  | First peak, right (N) | 20 N (3%) | ±55 N | 22–24 N (3%) | ±62 to ±66 N |
  | Second peak, left (N) | 16 N (2%) | ±45 N | 18–19 N (2–3%) | ±49 to ±53 N |
  | Second peak, right (N) | 16 N (2%) | ±43 N | 21–27 N (3–4%) | ±52 to ±59 N |
  | Time of first peak (% stance) | 1% (6–7%) | ±2 to ±3% | 1% | ±3% |
  | Time of second peak (% stance) | 1% (2%) | ±2% | 1% | ±0 to ±2% |

- Summary statements from the same paper: **SEM for force peaks was under 5% of the mean; kinetic MDC₉₅ was 4–20% between days**, versus 3–13% for temporal and 11–43% for spatial measures within-day. The narrative text summarises force-peak RC₉₅ as ±53 to ±63 N, which does not fully match the ±43 to ±66 N range in its own table — [PMC3766030](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3766030/)
- **Loading rate and impulse were not measured** in that reliability study — [PMC3766030](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3766030/)
- **Healthy children (capacitance-based instrumented treadmill):** peak vGRF detectable change ≈ **±3% during walking and ±11% during running**; running MDCs were roughly double walking MDCs — [Gait & Posture, Reliability and MDC of measures of gait in children on an instrumented treadmill (PubMed 31648119)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31648119/) / [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966636219307234)
- **Healthy seniors (integrated pressure-platform treadmill):** MDC for peak GRF ≈ **±0.1 body weight** during walking and running; SEM under 5% of the mean for gait parameters — [Reliability of gait parameters during treadmill walking in community-dwelling healthy seniors, Gait & Posture (ResearchGate record)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/224896407_Reliability_of_gait_parameters_during_treadmill_walking_in_community-_dwelling_healthy_seniors) / [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966636212001233)
- **Insole vs treadmill agreement:** treadmill GRF ICCs ≈ **0.82 for walking and 0.92 for running**; the treadmill showed more variance against an insole reference than a force plate did — [ISBS proceedings, Wireless insoles to measure ground reaction forces](https://commons.nmu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1268&=&context=isbs)
- **Step counts for stability:** reliability figures above were derived from 30 s captures averaging **52 ± 5 steps**; the paper makes no explicit recommendation on a minimum number of steps — [PMC3766030](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3766030/). Elsewhere, step-level variability (%CV) was computed over **40–60 steps per subject** — [PMC10781249](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781249/); and loading rate was averaged over **~20 strides per limb per speed** — [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/)
- **Normative reference datasets:** GaitRec (2,084 patients / 211 controls, 75,732 bilateral trials, vertical + AP + ML GRF and COP, 250 Hz released, BW-normalised, 101-point stance normalisation) is explicitly intended for "normative modeling, and tracking therapy progress" and for putting "assistive machine learning techniques into clinical gait analysis practice" — [PMC7217853](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217853/)

### Inferences
- **A defensible minimum detectable change for peak vGRF on a treadmill-style deck is ≈0.05–0.10 BW (≈5–7% of a ~1.1 BW peak) between sessions**, converging the ±43–66 N figure (≈±0.06–0.09 BW for a 75 kg adult), the ±0.1 BW senior figure, and the ±3% paediatric walking figure. Anything smaller than ~0.05 BW should not be reported as a real change.
- **Landmark timing is more reliable in relative terms for F3 than F1** (±0–2% vs ±2–3% of stance), consistent with F1's timing depending on a sharper, more variable heel-strike event.
- Running roughly **doubles** the MDC relative to walking (paediatric data). Any slow-running mode on a deck should widen its change-detection thresholds accordingly rather than reusing walking thresholds.
- ~40–60 steps per condition is the de facto standard for a stable per-session estimate, and ~20 strides per limb is the floor used for loading rate. A session summary built on fewer than ~20 steps per limb has no literature support.
- Because no MDC exists for impulse, loading rate or symmetry indices, **a device reporting those should derive and publish its own test-retest MDC**. Composite/ratio measures propagate the errors of both limbs, so an LSI MDC will necessarily be larger than a single-limb peak MDC — likely in the low tens of percent, given control ASI of 9% ± 7% and post-TKA LSI SDs of 12–14%.

### Gaps
- **No MDC for vertical impulse, loading rate, LSI, ASI, or any flattening/trough measure** was found in any retrieved source. This is the clearest measurement-science gap relevant to the assignment.
- The post-stroke treadmill MDC study surfaced in search but its numeric MDC values were not retrievable.
- A "healthy male adults, gait and half-squat vGRF MDC" paper exists but the search result flagged its SEM units as not matching a GRF measure; I did not retrieve it and would not cite its numbers without the original.

## Q9. Cumulative load / load dose as a rehabilitation metric

### Takeaway
There is a real and growing literature on cumulative load — load magnitude integrated or multiplied across a session, day or fortnight — but **no consensus definition, no standard units and no validated clinical thresholds**. The best-specified published implementations are (a) insole-measured **cumulative impulse above a threshold, in BW·hour**, (b) a **weighted sum of peak load and cumulative impulse** used to tier exercises, and (c) **Daily Load Stimulus**-family bone-adaptation models that weight cycle count against stress magnitude.

### Cited Findings
- **Most fully specified published implementation** (Achilles tendinopathy, insole-based, 20 Hz logging, up to two weeks, mean 10.1 ± 2.2 wear days, n = 15): cumulative load is "the numerically integrated impulse (×BW×hour) above a threshold", with **two thresholds — overall load ≥0.3×BW** (the load of a seated heel raise, the lowest-loading exercise) and **high-level load ≥3.0×BW** ("approximately the peak Achilles tendon load during walking") — [PMC12236876](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12236876/)
- Values from that study: **overall 0.98×BW per hour (SD 0.18; range 0.69–1.27); high-level 0.08×BW per hour (SD 0.07; range 0–0.21); non-normalised overall load 24.2×BW×hour (SD 15.7; range 8.1–62.1); non-normalised high-level 2.3×BW×hour (SD 2.8; range 0–9.3); time above 0.3×BW 24.1 hours (SD 14.3); time above 3.0×BW 0.6 hours (SD 0.7)** — [PMC12236876](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12236876/)
- Crucially, **the high-threshold measure outperformed the overall measure** for association with function: high-level load per hour vs double-leg heel raise height **r = 0.687**, vs fast isokinetic peak moment **r = 0.625** (both "strong", defined as ≥0.6), while overall load per hour reached only r = 0.543 against isometric peak moment and ≤0.413 elsewhere — [PMC12236876](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12236876/). **Caveat: medRxiv preprint, n = 15; the page states a peer-reviewed version was published in *Scientific Reports*, which I could not verify.**
- **Exercise-tiering loading index:** across 35 weight-bearing rehabilitation exercises and ADLs, "a loading index was computed via a weighted sum of loading peak and cumulative loading impulse for each exercise" with equal weights, and exercises were grouped into low / moderate / high tiers — [PMC10315869](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10315869/). This is patellofemoral-joint specific, not a per-session whole-limb dose.
- **Bone-adaptation (Daily Load Stimulus) family:** DLS "quantif[ies] daily stress histories by combining stress magnitude with the number of loading cycles" and is implemented in a wearable tibial-shock system that "take[s] into account the different effects of running, walking, standing, and recovery" — [US Patent 10405780](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10405780). Whalen's underlying model relates bone density to **daily cumulative peak cyclic effective stresses**, with a **stress exponent m** setting the trade-off between magnitude and cycle count — [search trail, Science.gov daily load stimulus topic page](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/d/daily+load+stimulus)
- **Cumulative-weighted tibial impulse** is in active use as an outcome: a military weight-carriage study estimated tibial loading via musculoskeletal modelling and beam theory, focusing on "peak tibial bending moments and cumulative-weighted tibial impulse", and found footwear modified it — [Footwear has a modifying effect on tibial loading during military weight carriage, Scientific Reports 2025 (PMC12280124)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12280124/)
- Cumulative load discriminates bone-stress-injury history: runners with recent BSI had "significantly higher peak tibial axial force, higher per step impulse, and higher cumulative load than controls" — [ISBS proceedings, Tibial load in runners with a history of bone stress injury](https://commons.nmu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2869&context=isbs). **n = 3 per group**; treat as hypothesis-generating only.
- Animal dose-response confirms cycle count matters independently of magnitude: murine tibial loading studies compared "a different number of continuous load cycles applied daily to each group (36, 216 and 1200)" — [PMC9344861](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9344861/)
- Cumulative load as an amputee/bone-anchored-limb outcome: "Cumulative loading increases and loading asymmetries persist during walking for people with a transfemoral bone-anchored limb" — [Gait & Posture 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0966636224001632)
- Device-vendor framing for scale: body-weight-support running patents estimate cumulative session load as force × duration, e.g. "a 200 lb runner's full-body-weight hour at about 20,000 lbs for the workout, and a partially unloaded session at 16,000 lbs" — [US Patent 12582873](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12582873). Device-specific estimates, not validated clinical metrics.
- Resistance-training analogue, for contrast: load management resources track "total weight lifted per session (sets × reps × resistance)" — [Absolute Balance, The Role of Load Management in Rehabilitation](https://www.absolutebalance.com.au/the-role-of-load-management-in-rehabilitation) (practitioner blog, low evidentiary weight)

### Inferences
- The **unit convention that is actually defensible for a cumulative vertical-load metric is BW·s (or BW·hour)** — i.e. summed impulse — optionally reported both as a raw total and as a rate (per hour of loaded time, or per step). PMC12236876 is the only retrieved source that specifies both and it reports both, which is the right pattern.
- **Thresholded cumulative load beats total cumulative load.** The single most actionable finding in this section is that a high-threshold cumulative measure correlated more strongly with function (r up to 0.687) than an unthresholded one (r ≤ 0.543). A deck should report cumulative impulse **above one or more clinically meaningful thresholds** (e.g. ≥0.5 BW for "took real weight", ≥1.0 BW for "full weight bearing", ≥1.5 BW for "impact-level"), not just a single grand total.
- The DLS/Whalen family implies cumulative load should be **magnitude-weighted with an exponent, not linearly summed**, if the intent is to represent bone or tissue stimulus rather than mechanical work. A linear impulse sum treats 10,000 low-force steps as equivalent to 1,000 high-force steps, which the animal and bone-modelling literature says is wrong. Any "bone stimulus" claim needs the exponent and must cite its source.
- A four-load-cell vertical deck can compute **per-step vertical impulse, step count, thresholded cumulative impulse, and per-limb cumulative totals** natively, which covers essentially every published cumulative-load construct except those requiring joint-level modelling (tibial bending moment, patellofemoral force, Achilles tendon load — all of which need segment kinematics and/or moment arms, not just Fz).

### Gaps
- **No consensus definition of a per-session cumulative weight-bearing dose, no standard units, and no validated clinical thresholds or progression rules** were found. Several targeted searches produced patents, device marketing, small preprints and joint-specific indices rather than a clinical standard.
- The JOSPT clinical-reasoning paper on optimal load for tibial and metatarsal bone stress injuries (jospt.2021.9982), which is the most likely source of published **load progression rules**, returned HTTP 403 and could not be read. It should be obtained directly — it is the most promising single unretrieved source for this question.
- No study found that links a cumulative weight-bearing dose measured in rehabilitation to a clinical outcome (healing, function, return to activity) in a post-surgical population.

## Cross-cutting: what a vertical-only (four-load-cell) deck can and cannot measure

### Takeaway
Every discrete landmark, rate, impulse, asymmetry and cumulative-load metric covered above is computable from Fz(t) alone. The measures that are **not** available are those built on the horizontal GRF components — braking/propulsive peaks and impulses, the AP weight-acceptance and propulsion variables, mediolateral force, and anything derived from the resultant force vector.

### Cited Findings — measures explicitly requiring non-vertical components [NOT VERTICAL-ONLY]
- **Braking vs propulsion function.** The THA paper's conclusion that "the operative side had better push off but worse braking functions" rests on the AP component; its Eq. 3 (`J = Σ GRFᵢ·dt`) is the horizontal impulse form — [PMC11608778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11608778/)
- **ADWA / ADPO** (anteroposterior weight acceptance and propulsion), used as primary outcomes in the knee OA flattening study: OA leg 0.23 ± 0.04 kg/BW vs control 0.28 ± 0.09 kg/BW — [arXiv 2510.16942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16942)
- **Speed-dependent AP forces.** "Fz1 and all the antero-posterior forces increased with the speed" — the AP forces are a distinct, speed-sensitive family unavailable from Fz — [search trail](https://www.academia.edu/125405308/Effects_of_age_and_walking_speeds_on_vertical_ground_reaction_force_in_younger_and_older_adults)
- **ML force and multiplanar asymmetry.** Direction-specific interlimb asymmetry after ACLR is studied with multiplanar force plates — [MDPI Applied Sciences 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/20/10284)
- GaitRec records "vertical, anterior-posterior, and medio-lateral ground reaction force, plus anterior-posterior and medio-lateral center of pressure" — so **only the vertical channel of a three-component reference dataset is directly comparable** to a vertical-only deck — [PMC7217853](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7217853/)
- Braking/propulsive impulse and stance time were all reported as speed-dependent in running — [Variations in ground reaction force parameters at different running speeds](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0167945783900052)

### Inferences
- **Available from Fz alone:** F1, F2, F3 magnitudes and timings; stance time; contact/toe-off events (50 N threshold convention, [PMC9288487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9288487/)); average and instantaneous vertical loading rate; vertical unloading/push-off rate; total and sub-phase vertical impulse; waveform flattening / trough depth; step count; all LSI/ASI variants of any of the above; thresholded cumulative vertical impulse; double-support and single-support load sharing when both limbs are instrumented.
- **A four-load-cell deck also yields centre-of-pressure position** by moment balance across the four vertical cells, even though it cannot measure horizontal *force*. COP excursion (AP and ML) is therefore available and is part of what GaitRec distributes. This is a frequently overlooked capability and worth stating explicitly — but COP position is a different quantity from AP/ML force and cannot substitute for it.
- **Not available:** braking and propulsive peak forces and impulses; AP weight-acceptance (ADWA) and propulsion (ADPO); mediolateral force; the resultant GRF vector magnitude and orientation; friction/shear measures; and any inverse-dynamics joint moment, which requires both the full force vector and segment kinematics. Claims about "push-off *function*" vs "braking *function*", or about joint loading (patellofemoral force, knee adduction moment, tibial bending moment, Achilles tendon load), are therefore out of reach without additional instrumentation.
